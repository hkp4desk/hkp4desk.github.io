The Church Is Not Outside the Law: A Mass for the Wang Fuk Court Dead Is Proper, Insinuation Is Not

14 November 2026The HKP4 DeskProjectionProspect

中文版本 Chinese version →

On 14 November Ming Pao led its Hong Kong section with a letter from Cardinal Stephen Chow, Bishop of the Catholic Diocese of Hong Kong. On 13 November he wrote to the parishes and asked that on 26 November, the first anniversary of the Wang Fuk Court fire, they “offer a Mass for the dead of Tai Po’s Wang Fuk Court” (back-translated). The prayer issued the same day by the diocesan liturgy commission, after asking comfort for the dead and their families, asks the faithful to be taught to “practise benevolence, hold to the truth, and do justice” (back-translated). Father Joseph Ha, parish priest of Our Lady of Mount Carmel Church in Wan Chai, said the letter had “steadied everyone’s direction” in the wind and rain (back-translated), that the church should “speak the needs of the people” (back-translated), and that the “police friends” (back-translated) who had come to the church earlier had not followed up, he did not know whether opinion had done the work. This journal’s position first: a Mass for these 168 dead is entirely proper, and so are religious faith and a person’s mourning. What is not proper is to write mourning as insinuation, and to treat the church as a place where one may hint at the government without finishing the sentence.

A Mass for the dead is entirely proper.

November is the Catholic month of the Holy Souls. Ming Pao of 11 November reported that Father Ha had already said on Commercial Radio that the parish prays for the dead every year, that if someone asks prayers for Wang Fuk Court the parish will pray, that there is “no need to add and no need to subtract” (back-translated), and that the prayer is for everyone, “each person included, none left out” (back-translated). Cardinal Chow’s letter fixes the day on the first anniversary of the Wang Fuk Court fire and asks that later Sunday Masses add an intention for the dead. It is still that one act. The 168 lives are not a topic to be used for an essay. Families need a place where the names are read aloud. A church that gives them that place is doing what a church is for. On this part, this journal enters no reservation.

The reservation sits in the second half of the prayer. HK01 the same day quoted the liturgy commission’s text. The first half asks “the merciful God to continue to comfort the souls of the dead and grant them eternal rest, and to comfort every family, so that the living may know the Lord’s mercy and love” (back-translated). Those lines match mourning. What follows, “we also ask the Lord to teach us to practise benevolence, hold to the truth, and do justice” (back-translated), is different. Benevolence can still be a pastor speaking to the faithful. The letter does not say which fact already on the record this truth refers to, nor which proceeding now under way this justice refers to. Before the first anniversary of the Wang Fuk Court fire, the inquiry report, the accounting of the departments, and the criminal case against the contractors each have a text and a date. If the prayer means these, writing them down is hope inside mourning. If the prayer does not mean these, and yet sends the six characters out inside a news story about law-enforcement officers “showing concern” (back-translated) for a church, a reader can finish the sentence in only one way: that the Hong Kong government does not hold to the truth and does not do justice. If that is the claim, say it to a person’s face, and name the page and the item that failed the dead. To hide it in a prayer, and let the newspaper complete the hint, is covert opposition. It is not prayer.

Father Ha told Ming Pao that Cardinal Chow had been away from Hong Kong, and that the letter “steadied everyone’s direction” in the wind and rain, for “in the wind and rain, a leader has to come out and speak; I appreciate him” (back-translated). A bishop writing to his parishes and fixing the Mass for the first anniversary of the Wang Fuk Court fire is doing his office. Steadying a direction is not the problem. What he did not say is where the wind and rain come from. He went on: residents are alert and concerned about this fire, and the church, living in society, must “speak the needs of the people” (back-translated). Three “police friends” had come to Our Lady of Mount Carmel to ask about the Mass, and there was no follow-up; he did “not know whether opinion had done it” (back-translated). Ming Pao’s subhead was “Priest praises a direction steadied in the wind and rain” (back-translated). The paper set the Mass for that fire anniversary inside the story of officers showing concern for a church, then used his mouth to read a pastoral letter as a hand on the tiller. A hand on the tiller is needed only if a storm is blowing at the church. The letter does not write whom the storm names, and the interview does not mention it either.

Do “truth” and “justice” mean the government, and where does the storm come from and whom does it name?

The church is protected by the Basic Law, and it is bound by the Basic Law. Article 32 gives Hong Kong residents freedom of religious belief, and freedom to preach and to conduct and join religious activities in public. Article 141 says the government shall not interfere in the internal affairs of religious organisations, and it limits that protection to religious activities which do not contravene the laws of Hong Kong. The two clauses are two sides of one rule. A Mass may be offered in public. A church is not a place outside the law. On 14 November the Commissioner of Police, Joe Chow, told HK01 that for a public event the police have always had a duty to understand it with the organiser, that whether it goes ahead is a question for the organiser, and that the principle is that it be lawful. Ming Pao of 11 November recorded that the three “friends” (back-translated) asked how many people would come, made no threat, and left when they had asked. He said there is no reason to stop the annual Mass of the month of the Holy Souls, and that “what is civil is civil, what is religious is religious, and the normal life of religion is not to be interfered with” (back-translated). This journal agrees that the normal life of religion is not to be interfered with. That same life cannot be used as a screen for insinuation. Asking the expected size of a public gathering is not a ban on prayer. To rename the question a storm, and to rename the lack of a follow-up a victory of opinion, is to recast a lawful inquiry as suppression, so that insinuation has a hero’s place to stand.

The same story gives the Anglican arrangements as fact and then stops. A worship service early in the month read out the names of the Wang Fuk Court dead, including the firefighter Ho Wai-ho. The notice and the official paper Echo say that the service at St John’s Cathedral in Central on 28 November is for prayer and reflection for residents affected by the fire over the past year and for those who walked with them, “and to seek hope together in the Lord” (back-translated). Ming Pao stops at that sentence. It does not ask what the hope is aimed at, just as it does not ask whom truth and justice name. Two religious events sit in one story. One is written as a direction in the storm. The other is written as a notice. If the standard is mourning, both should be written as mourning. If the standard is a sentence that does not finish, both should be asked to finish it.

A clergyman who wants to do something for Hong Kong has a wide road in front of him. Families still need company after the fire, and residents still in temporary housing need someone beside them. The regulations for old-building maintenance, the accountability of the incorporated owners’ companies, the progress of compensation and rehousing, the order of the testing and the inquiry: every one of these can be worked on, and every one of them benefits somebody once it is done. Such causes pay poorly in attention. They do not sound as fine as a letter steadying direction in the storm, and a newspaper may not lead with them again. Working and speaking are two roads. When speech lands on a concrete matter, it is the pastor’s duty, and this journal supports it. When speech steps around every concrete matter and leaves only an accusation with no name and a storm nobody will describe, the speaker has left the altar and taken another stage. On that stage the 168 names are no longer flesh and blood; they are props. The sorest topic in the city becomes a footing; the winning line becomes the hint that names no one; a lawful inquiry is written as suppression, the absence of a follow-up as a victory of opinion, and black and white turn over. The wound is not closed; it is torn open one more time. The name comes out of that tear, and the profit comes out of that tear. The father in Father is the man who undertakes to look after the orphan. To raise himself on the grief of orphans, however neat his vestments, is not to be a father; it is to be a charlatan.

When the disaster came, the charlatans were not there. In the first days after the fire was out, the people going into the blocks to count what was left were volunteers; the ones registering families in the community hall and sitting with them at night were social workers and neighbours. The people carrying supplies, cleaning the temporary housing, and knocking on doors to bring the elderly their medicine never appeared in the paper. Lawyers read the owners’ company documents for nothing, engineers inspected buildings for nothing, accountants helped small shops work out their compensation; none of it makes a headline. When people were making proposals, patching the system, and planning what comes next, the charlatans were again not there. Mandatory building inspection, a review of the subcontracting system, a public timetable for compensation and rehousing: those voices have been in the district councils, in the legislature, and in the reports of the professional bodies, with a text behind every one of them; they simply do not come with a storm attached. Where then are the charlatans? They were waiting. They wait for the anniversary, for the coverage, for the window in which the six characters can reach a front page. When the window opens they read out the lines they wrote for themselves; in all the days the window stayed shut, not one of the things above was produced by waiting. Mourning has its dates; the work has no dates. The charlatans appear on the days with cameras; the workers are there every day.

The 168 dead need their names remembered. The families need rest and comfort. The first half of the prayer has already said so, and it should be done as written. If the second half is a separate charge, the church door does not spare it a public test. In Hong Kong the church may speak for the dead, may speak for the families, and may say, on the facts, that a particular piece of the government’s work was not done. A criticism needs a subject, an object, and evidence. Justice with no subject is not justice. It leaves the listener to fill in the person being accused.

The church is not outside the law, and still less may it carry on covert opposition by insinuation. If there is something to say, say it, and say it plainly. Neither side need cover its tracks or deny the silver buried under its own floor. There is nothing so sordid that it cannot bear the light. To use one’s influence to open a psychological rift in society, and then to present that rift as a service to society, is loyalty wearing the face of treachery.

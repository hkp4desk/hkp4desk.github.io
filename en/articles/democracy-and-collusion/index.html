The Lai and Wong Cases: Which Step of Asking a Foreign Power to Sanction Your Own Country Was Democratic? The Concept-Swapping Show Has to End

12 November 2026The HKP4 DeskProjectionProspect

中文版本 Chinese version →

On 15 December 2025 a panel of three National Security Law designated judges of the High Court found Next Digital founder Jimmy Lai guilty on all counts: one count of conspiring to publish seditious publications and two counts of conspiring with foreign or external forces to endanger national security. The judgment called him the mastermind of the conspiracy. On 9 February 2026 he was jailed for 20 years, the heaviest sentence imposed under the law. On 5 November this year Joshua Wong pleaded guilty, under the same statute, to conspiring with foreign forces and was sentenced to 10 years. The courts tried these cases; within a week of the sentencing a different crowd rushed to file its own verdicts, in wording so uniform it might have come off one press. Human Rights Watch, in a statement issued the day sentence was passed, called Wong “a well-known democracy activist”, demanded his “immediate and unconditional release”, and urged foreign governments to condemn the case and to “impose targeted sanctions on officials responsible”. Former US House Speaker Pelosi called the sentence “an appalling abuse of power” and enrolled Wong, Lai and other prisoners together as “political prisoners”. Nathan Law, writing in The Guardian, said Beijing meant to “crush every hope for democracy”. Benny Rogers of Hong Kong Watch published in three outlets inside one week: make releases a priority in London’s China policy, lean on Beijing in Washington, keep the King out of China before the prisoners are free, let the Pope speak up. A British member of the House of Lords questioned the Foreign Office about Lai; the Foreign Secretary’s half-yearly report on Hong Kong named Lai and Wong. The EU’s External Action Service issued a statement, and ten foreign ministers followed jointly the next day. The European branch of the China Democracy Party asked European governments to name these cases in every high-level contact with Beijing. Rallies gathered outside Chinese consulates in Vancouver, Toronto and Calgary, with a federal MP attending to call for releases. Another organisation demanded to know why the Harvard Kennedy School still hosted a talk on “one country, two systems”.

These voices differ in passport, title and party line, yet share one sentence pattern: guilty because of democracy, therefore the trial itself is the suppression of democracy.

Democracy and collusion are two different things, and the definition comes first. As an institution, democracy carries a minimal meaning on which all its theorists agree: a rule-bound way of settling arguments inside one political community. The majority decides, minority rights are protected, and tonight’s loser may contest the majority again in the next round. The whole method rests on one premise: the quarrel is a household quarrel, the stakes are household stakes, the referee is the household’s own rules. The democratic procedure protects all of these: ballots, speech, assembly, association, an opposition that may one day become the government. One thing was never inside the protection: stepping outside the household’s rules to invite an outside power to strike at the household itself. A democratic answer is counted in votes; a request for foreign sanctions hands the answer to a foreign government’s executive order. Change the unit of measurement and you have changed the thing measured.

This line is not a Hong Kong specialty; Western statute books draw it just as firmly. The United States has prosecuted under its Espionage Act of 1917 for more than a century: handing a foreign power information connected with the national defence carries up to ten years, and both Assange and Snowden were charged under that law. Britain’s National Security Act 2023 created a foreign interference offence carrying up to fourteen years, and in June 2026 two men who stalked and surveilled residents of Hong Kong background in Britain on behalf of Chinese intelligence services were sentenced under it to eight years and ten years. In neither country did such cases draw a single statement about persecution, and nobody thought to reach for the word democracy. What you think of your own government is politics; what you conspire to do with a foreign government against your own country is criminal law. Holding the two apart is the common bottom line of every national security statute.

Now look at what the courts actually found. Lai’s judgment runs 855 pages, built from 156 days of trial and read out in court item by item: he deployed his personal international network, and through the Chan Kwong team lobbied the parliaments of multiple countries to impose sanctions on the governments and officials of China and Hong Kong; the judges found that under the pretext of helping Hong Kong people he sought American assistance in order, in their words, to bring down China’s government (back-translated); his aide Mark Simon arranged his two trips to Washington in 2019 and meetings with a US senator, and was described in the judgment as a shadowy figure moving between the American government and Lai. The Apple Daily companies were likewise convicted and fined $3.0045 million each; eight co-defendants, among them former chief executive Keung Kin-hung and former editor-in-chief Law Wai-kwong, received between six years three months and ten years, five of them testifying as accessory witnesses. Set the facts out by their centre of gravity: meetings, requests, lists, teams, funds, an organised course of conduct aimed at making foreign governments act against one’s own country.

Not one step in that chain was a ballot.

The Wong case is simpler, because he admitted it himself. If what he did were merely democratic activity, there would have been nothing to admit. No law punishes a thought, and what a defendant pleads guilty to in court is never a view but the conduct inside the word conspiracy: approaching foreign officials and asking them to sanction one’s own country. Those who shout loudest for Joshua Wong’s release owe one answer first: of all the conduct he admitted in his own mouth, which item belongs to democratic activity? Nobody answers, because the answer cuts the other way: on the same standard, the two men who stalked people in London for a foreign intelligence service were democrats too.

Spread these statements across one table and the first thing you notice is an absence: in one week there were twelve statements, columns, replies and communiqués, plenty of talk about the verdicts and not one of them speaking from the judgment. Elaine Pearson’s label for Wong is an influential democratic leader, and her remedy is targeted sanctions. Pelosi’s statement never touches the facts found in court; it promises never to forget the prisoners it calls political prisoners. Law, writing as a political refugee, quotes the judgment’s finding that he and Wong were the case’s two principal conspirators, and quotes it to prove the prosecution’s political character; the same judgment’s itemized record of meetings, requests and lists goes unanswered in his column. Rogers’s six-point programme ranges from sanctioning Beijing to briefing kings and popes, and every point steps around the same question: what conduct the courts had actually tried.

This is a concept-swapping show performed badly, and one sleight of hand performs three trades here. The first swaps conduct for advocacy: the judgments record meetings, requests, lists and money; the statements rename all of it “calls”, “voices” and “the exercise of freedoms”. The second swaps accomplices for the international community: when foreign officials receive the requests and act on them, this language stops calling it collusion and starts calling it international concern. The third swaps private interest for human rights: once the fortunes of one’s own side are invoked in the name of values, whoever examines the conduct is accused of persecuting the values. The neatest mirror is held up by Human Rights Watch itself. In the same statement it condemns Hong Kong for punishing collusion under the security law and urges foreign governments to impose targeted sanctions on Hong Kong and mainland officials. If soliciting foreign sanctions is the conduct of this offence, the statement is itself the same conduct; if it is no offence, on what basis was anyone convicted? The question goes unanswered because the questioners never meant to ask it of themselves. London’s own paperwork supplies the comparison: the written reply its foreign office filed on 9 November records that its minister, visiting Hong Kong on 29 October, raised Lai’s continued detention while meeting representatives of the Hong Kong government and of China’s foreign ministry. By the logic of these statements, a foreign government delivering its views in person is a far more substantial intervention than any press release.

The irony is the double face of one and the same script. When Britain jailed two men on its own soil for running a foreign intelligence service’s errands, not one government issued a statement about London suppressing a community. When the United States charged Snowden under the Espionage Act, no statement accused Washington of crushing freedom. When the case is theirs, the offence is called national security and the courtrooms are independent; when the case is Hong Kong’s, the offence is renamed the suppression of democracy and the courts become a tool. Mr Rubio’s statement on sentencing day called the 20 years an unjust and tragic conclusion and urged the authorities to grant Lai humanitarian parole. And in no country on earth does a foreign ministry send a telegram deciding who may walk out of a local court’s prison early. A standard that changes with the passport is not defending democracy; it is using democracy as a prop of geopolitics.

The premise, guilty for democracy, has never been proved by anybody. The convictions turned on conduct; the 47-page sentencing reasons measure criminal responsibility item by item against the acts and their effects, and the judgment restates that the punishment must fit the gravity of the crime. Sentencing faces the harm of acts, not the identity of the defendant and certainly not the mood of foreign politicians. Once the premise fails, the conclusion has nothing left to stand on but feeling. The word democracy is neither a pass nor a screen. The appeal route stands open by law, the judgment and the sentencing reasons are published in full on the Judiciary’s website, and whoever believes the courts got it wrong has the proper road: take the facts and the law into court and argue. That road has never been closed, and whoever says it is closed should bring the evidence. A logic a schoolchild could follow, that conduct is conduct and opinion is opinion and collusion is neither, was feigned not to be understood for a week, not because it is deep but because sentiment and calculation sell only while the confusion lasts. Recasting a trial of conduct as a trial of opinion, re-labelling accomplices as the international community: the audience is thinning, and the players still move from venue to venue with the same lines. We print the line, one clause each:

The argument of democracy belongs inside the house; the conspiracy of collusion belongs outside it. The courts convicted the second, and never the first.

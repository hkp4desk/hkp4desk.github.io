Wang Fuk Court: True Responsibility May Lie in Thinking About How to Move Forward

8 November 2026The HKP4 DeskProsperityProjectionProspect

中文版本 Chinese version →

On 26 November 2025 a grade-five fire tore through eight residential towers at Wang Fuk Court in Tai Po. One hundred and sixty-eight people died; 1,984 households lost their homes. The year that followed was spent in rescue, inquiry and resettlement. On 29 October 2026, the 337th day after the fire, Chief Executive John Lee Ka-chiu opened a press conference by leading his bureau secretaries and department heads in a bow: “On behalf of the HKSAR Government, I express our apology to the affected residents and to the public at large” (back-translated). The same day the inquiry report was published, a document seven months in the making, built on 30 public hearings, 80 witnesses and more than a million files. The government announced it would accept all 120 recommendations in full; Housing Secretary Winnie Ho Wing-yin had handed in her resignation the day before to bear political responsibility, and the Central Government approved her removal on 3 November. Apology, accountability and reform duly delivered, and the argument should by rights have quietened; it has not. Criticism has not paused since the day of that press conference, and it reduces to three claims. The first: accountability is too thin, one secretary falling is a mere “minimum charge”, nowhere near covering “the whole family”. The second: the truth is incomplete, at least 41 pages of the report were blacked out, the building inspection reports are still withheld, and the government dares not lay everything on the table. The third: the reform has no sincerity, all of it is said to be a script, public relations, a device to let the story cool. Taken separately, each claim has a measure of reason. After a disaster that killed 168 people, a society without criticism would be the anomaly, and this journal has no wish to stop its ears. The trouble starts when the three are merged, because each holds only one half of the facts, and the missing half is what best exposes them. Measured by how much of the residents’ real problems have been solved and how many of the city’s safety gaps have been closed, the government’s course over this year has been the right one: it helps the displaced climb out of grief, and it helps Hong Kong keep moving forward efficiently. What follows proceeds in two steps: first set the facts out in full, then place each of the three claims before those facts and see which ones can stand.

The first fact is relief and resettlement. What the government has been thinking about for these households all year is “how to live tomorrow”, and the answer sits entirely in numbers. The buyout price runs roughly 30 per cent above the valuation the Hong Kong Institute of Surveyors drew from pre-fire market transactions. On 25 August the Housing Bureau’s spokesman put the reasoning on the record: this is a special arrangement for a fire of a scale and depth “unprecedented in Hong Kong”. By the 31 August deadline, 1,967 households, or 99.1 per cent of owners, had returned their signed confirmations of sale; by 29 October, 94.8 per cent had executed formal agreements, figures the Deputy Financial Secretary, Wong Wai-lun, gave the following evening. The designated sale scheme holds more than 4,400 flats in ten Housing Authority and Housing Society projects, priced between about HK$1.5 million and just over HK$5.3 million; balloting began in late September and by mid-October more than 350 households had picked their new homes. The rental allowance is a flat HK$300,000 over two years, extended until three months after move-in, and every household has its own social worker. In the columns that have carried the criticism, these numbers are almost nowhere to be found.

Beyond the numbers there are people. Recovery does not keep a press-conference calendar. After every great disaster anywhere in the world, grief takes a long time to recede and some people never fully leave the shadow; this is the common experience of human beings facing death. Sorrow keeps its own time, and the word “recovery” should not have its deadline set by outsiders on anyone’s behalf. A new flat cannot return the dead, a subsidy cannot buy back a relative, and every household that takes the resettlement package also carries the helplessness of having to go on living. What we hope to see all the same is this: those who cannot leave the shadow at least have solid ground beneath their feet; however hard it is, keep walking toward a better life. The first thing to set down is rancour. The dead would hardly wish their families to spend the rest of their lives brimming with resentment, backs turned to the sunlight, and rancour, once loosed, always cuts the living first. Life was never easy; those walking forward may look more at the sunlight. The government must understand this and so must society, and the record already contains the words. The inquiry report’s conclusion prints 168 names beneath a prayer that the dead may rest in peace and the living find strength (back-translated); Ms Ho’s departure statement expressed the hope that the departed may rest, the families move on untroubled, and the pain be walked out of soon. Official text and private wish turn out to be the same sentence. On this line there is no opposition, only a community.

Take the first claim first, and take it from the people making it. On the afternoon of 29 October, Mr Yip Ka-kau, a resident of Wang Tai House who lost his wife in the fire, told reporters that a solo exit by Ms Ho was mere “minimum charge”; the image was his own, in Cantonese flavoured with English: precise accountability, he said, is like dining at a fine restaurant with a minimum charge, and this resignation is that charge. The next day, in an interview carried by Initium Media on 30 October, Mr So Hiu-fung, who lost both his octogenarian parents in the fire, asked furiously: “Why is it that only one secretary has fallen? Does one cover the whole family?” (back-translated). And on 28 October the South China Morning Post ran an opinion piece arguing that the secretaries of all four named departments should resign to take responsibility, and it set the test in these words: “If no minister has been held accountable by next month, when TV screens and newspapers are full of Tai Po commemorations, we will know the system has broken down.” There are two things inside these statements, and it helps to take them apart. The grief of the bereaved is not hard to imagine whoever is placed inside it; when a family member is gone, the wish to see more people answer for it is the most natural thought after loss. We have heard it, and we have only understanding and respect for it. But feeling is feeling and institutions are institutions. Between “more secretaries should go” and the severity of the disaster there is no necessary correspondence; the demand extends from grief and fury rather than following from the logic of accountability. Under the principal officials’ accountability system, political accountability weighs jurisdiction and institutions, not headcount. The fire fell within the remit of the Independent Checking Unit under the Housing Bureau, and the Chief Executive said in terms that although the fire involved no personal wrongdoing by the Housing Secretary, she should bear political responsibility (back-translated); from the resignation letter to the request for removal and the Central Government’s approval, the sequence matches, step for step, the accountability framework for principal officials established in 2002. What would the alternative be? Even the political scientist Lau Siu-kai has put it plainly: the authorities cannot let society feel they are simply finding a few people to offer up as scapegoats (back-translated). The insistence that more careers must end before accountability counts as enough turns political accountability into a headcount, which is precisely the logic of sacrifice the system exists to guard against. And accountability has not stopped with one person. On the criminal track, seven individuals and two companies face charges of manslaughter and conspiracy to defraud, and the cases continue. On the administrative track, the Public Service Commission has opened a second-tier inquiry to dispose of 40 to 50 cases within three months, with some 100 officers interviewed. On the political track, a secretary has left. Three tracks running at once, which is why the Liaison Office of the Central People’s Government judged the accountability across the three levels serious, conscientious and unprecedented in force (back-translated). That was not an empty phrase. If all this is still a “minimum charge”, then what the critics want is not proportionate accountability but unbounded accountability, and the latter has never been in the residents’ interest; it serves only what bystanders’ feelings require.

Now the second claim, that the truth is incomplete. By Initium Media’s page-by-page count, at least 41 pages of the published report were blacked out, and the building inspection reports have not been released; the criticism starts there. The question is not without its answer, and the strongest version comes from the bar. Writing in the Hong Kong 01 legal-affairs column, the barrister Lo Tin-yan argued that redaction protects a fair trial, follows the precedent set by the Lamma Island ferry disaster inquiry, and is required by the presumption of innocence under Article 87 of the Basic Law, with the full report to be published the moment the criminal proceedings conclude. The Housing Bureau’s reason for withholding the inspection reports is likewise on the record: the material may bear on future fire-insurance claims to be negotiated with the insurers. Set the statute beside the sentiment and the difference shows itself. What is covered are the prosecution’s evidentiary materials; releasing them all early would have the cases tried in the media first, and the defendants’ right to defence would have to yield to the headlines. The presumption of innocence is a constituent part of the rule of law, and on this point the critics and the government are not in disagreement at all. Nor does the complaint that the public’s right to know has gone short have much to stand on: a report of 1,013 pages in 16 chapters and 9 annexes, 30 public hearings, the senior management of the main contractor and of its engineering consultant named in the report as chiefly responsible and bound to answer item by item in open court. On this scale, the open record already amounts to the largest right-to-know exercise in Hong Kong’s disaster history.

The second claim cannot be judged, either, without the dispute over 13 households. The government’s response came on two levels. On attitude, refusing to sell one’s interest does not make anyone a saboteur who uses disaster to destabilise the city, and the Security Secretary, Chris Tang Ping-keung, said so on 30 June, before the argument even started. On procedure, with the deadline past the remaining interests return to the market mechanism, and the Deputy Financial Secretary said twice, on 17 September and 31 October, that the government will legislate next year to acquire the outstanding interests with compensation under law, after consultation with the Legislative Council. As for the other 94.8 per cent of households, agreements are signed household by household and new flats are chosen flat by flat; no one’s compensation is being held back by anyone. What is actually being held up is the demolition of the seven towers, since the authorities assess long-term demolition as highly likely, and the fate of the site cannot wait for the words “first gather all the owners”. One commentary has warned that acquisition by legislation “easily sets a precedent for depriving private property”. Behind that warning stand, however, 1,984 households who have already crossed the rubble and are walking forward. If the interests of 13 households can leave the homes of thousands indefinitely in limbo, is that fair? The government has, moreover, reportedly prepared its answer to possible judicial review: acquisition under law, compensation open to challenge, courts open to go to. If this is deprivation of private property, it is at least a deprivation visible in legal text and arguable in court. What has genuinely lived for years outside regulation is another trade entirely, the one behind bid-rigging and proxy votes that gambles the fortune of the whole estate to make its profit.

The last claim is the script theory, and it considers itself the most far-sighted. It belongs to no single person but is the stock script of the criticism trade since October. Newspaper editorials have warned on repeat that reform born of murky accountability drifts into public relations that turn the eye elsewhere; one paper set a test line for the anniversary and sat waiting for the show. On the day the report was submitted, the commentator Wu Enwei wrote the script out most plainly: set up a review committee, issue some technical guidelines, pursue a few front-line staff, then declare that the lessons have been learned (back-translated). The lines came first; all that remained was for the government to read them. The facts, however, refused to play their parts. All 120 recommendations were accepted. Bid-rigging becomes a criminal offence carrying up to 10 years’ imprisonment and a HK$10 million fine, brought within the Organised and Serious Crimes Ordinance and the ICAC’s list of specified offences; the Secretary for Justice, Paul Lam Ting-kwok, explained at the 29 October conference that interception, restraint of assets and forfeiture of criminal proceeds become available from here on. A new bureau for building maintenance services takes over the Urban Renewal Authority’s assisted-tendering service in January; the discredited Independent Checking Unit is abolished and replaced by a building-control unit for public housing; a central data system is under construction, with the Chief Secretary for Administration chairing a steering committee that reviews implementation item by item. On 5 and 6 November the joint task force of the police, the ICAC and the Competition Commission made its first strike, codenamed Luen Fung: 28 people linked to bid-rigging rings were arrested and, across the operation’s targets, 66 in all by the official count, while five construction contracts worth over HK$90 million were intercepted before signature. If that is “technical guidelines”, the guidelines cost 10 years; if that is “a few front-line staff”, the people in custody are directors of the main contractor, directors of the consultant and licensed professionals who lent their names. The report’s conclusion says: “The fire has been put out, but the reform has only just begun.” That sentence is the truth of it, and here is exactly the starting point the critics refuse to wait for. The test line drawn for the anniversary has gone unclaimed before its day; the foreign media left in a body in early November, and the agenda returned to enforcement and execution. The heat receded; the process did not. What cooled was the enthusiasm in front of cameras; what has begun to accelerate is the reform itself. And a Hong Kong functioning normally and efficiently is precisely the background the displaced need most in rebuilding their lives. A city paralysed by the streets and kidnapped by postures could not have saved a single resident.

This debate has in truth been running two timetables. One is filled from morning to night with flat balloting, legislation, penalties, the central data system, cell after cell, every one an answer to how 1,984 households will live from here. The other cell by cell records how many people must fall, how many pages were blacked out, how many days late an apology was, each aimed at the row of seats behind the press-conference table. One fire, two timetables: one filled with 168 names and 1,984 homes; the other with the headcount of resignations and the page count of redactions. Both filed under the same date, and long since not entries about the same thing. At this moment some owners are signing provisional sale-and-purchase agreements for their new flats, and some are still wrestling with lawyers over legal aid. Each has its difficulty, and each is being handled, bit by bit. As for the ink spent circling those seats behind the table: when this page turns, it will hurry on to the next subject.

Criticism in Hong Kong has never been unfree. The demand for “stricter accountability” has filled the newspapers, never banned, and the government has responded to it item by item with policy. But speech has a floor, and the floor is drawn by law: a disaster is not material, and the public’s grief is not fuel. As for who has been turning a tragedy into a business behind the scenes, the court record will ask plainly on the public’s behalf.

“The best tribute to the dead is to engrave the lessons; the best reflection on the disaster is to drive reform.” The Liaison Office put it that way, and it is this journal’s judgment too. What comes next: the three bills criminalising bid-rigging go to the Legislative Council; the Public Service Commission’s inquiry reports within three months; the outstanding interests of some 90 households move forward under law. The numbers will update item by item, the way the keys for the first 350 households have been handed over flat by flat. Critics want tears; the displaced want a new life. This city made its choice long ago: bury the dead, take the living by the hand, and walk toward the sunlight.

Next-week review point: the progress of the three bills criminalising bid-rigging through the Legislative Council; whether the Public Service Commission reports within the promised three months, by the end of January 2027; where the refusal of the 13 households and the roughly 90 unresolved interests end up; the updated count of households balloted; whether the blacked-out portions are released, as promised, when the criminal proceedings conclude. Let us see what kind of clumsy performance these people put on next.

Wang Fuk Court at One Year: The Missing Pieces of the Puzzle, Completed in Court

10 November 2026The HKP4 DeskProsperityProjectionProspect

中文版本 Chinese version →

On 10 November the political desk of the Orient Daily put an interview on its front page: five Wang Fuk Court residents and bereaved relatives, speaking from the heart almost a year after the fire. Its headline spoke of “168 wronged lives awaiting vindication” (back-translated). The grievances, sorted, are three. Four departments — the Fire Services Department, the Buildings Department, the Housing Bureau’s Independent Checking Unit and the Labour Department — were found derelict in duty, yet only one bureau director resigned: are the others free of responsibility, the residents asked? The inquiry report, in one resident’s phrase, is “a jigsaw with several pieces missing” (back-translated), and the truth of the fire remains incomplete. And the apology rang hollow: the Chief Executive “expressed” an apology, which in the ears of the bereaved is not the same as offering one. Tears, sarcasm, a voice breaking: the reporter set down plainly what was said. This journal’s position first: these words are to be heard seriously and answered point by point, neither evaded nor defended on the government’s behalf. “Awaiting vindication” waits upon a miracle; procedure does not believe in miracles, only in the next step. A wrong is set right through process. Where the puzzle lost its pieces, and who now holds them; and if the sincerity of an apology cannot be found in wording, it must be looked for in what is done next.

Begin with accountability. Mr Lee, a bereaved relative, asked furiously why, with four departments named in the report, only one minister fell: “then 50 civil servants could all be one department’s responsibility!” (back-translated) The feeling behind the sarcasm is understandable. When family is gone, the wish to see more people answer for it is the most natural of thoughts, and it deserves nothing but understanding and respect. Beyond the feeling, the system keeps its own measure, a point this journal set out last time: political accountability weighs jurisdiction, not headcount. The Independent Checking Unit sits under the Housing Bureau; in the Chief Executive’s own words, “the fire did not involve personal wrongdoing by the Secretary for Housing, but the Secretary for Housing should bear political responsibility” (back-translated), and from the resignation letter to Beijing’s approval the process followed the accountability system’s text. If accountability were counted in heads, no number would ever be enough. Seen in layers, each tier answers its own question. The secretary answered for jurisdiction, and has answered. Principal officers, division heads and front-line staff answer for duty: the two-level inquiry under the departmental-head accountability mechanism has begun under the Civil Service Commission’s inquiry regulations, with some 40 to 50 civil servants as its main subjects and three months on its clock; none of the four named departments escapes, and each owes its own account. Contractors and consultants answer for criminal liability, and that is in court. Read in reverse, Mr Lee’s sarcasm describes exactly the road the inquiry is walking: department by department, post by post, duty by duty. The four departments’ responsibility is being accounted for in layers; it is not being evaded.

Next, truth. “The whole jigsaw is missing several pieces” — Mr Yip’s line is the sharpest sentence in the interview, and the judgment is sound: the report exists, and it is indeed not the whole truth. But Ms Lo, a regular at the hearings, in effect said where the pieces went: the contractors and consultants implicated in bid-rigging carry criminal exposure and do not appear at hearings, so the commission could not reach the key evidence. This is not a failure of effort but a line drawn by law; the commission itself stated that there was “not sufficient basis to convert this hearing into a commission of inquiry” (back-translated), and evidence of a criminal grade lies beyond a hearing body’s reach. The missing pieces are not hidden; they are queued in procedural order. Seven individuals and two companies, the main contractor and the project consultant among them, stand charged with manslaughter and conspiracy to defraud, and the case returns to court on 14 January. The joint task force’s operation Luen Fung arrested 28 people linked to bid-rigging and stopped five construction contracts worth more than HK$90 million in total. At least 41 redacted pages of the report lift the moment criminal proceedings end. As the barrister Lo Tin-on has explained, the masking protects a fair trial, following the precedent set by the South Lantau ferry disaster inquiry. The report itself notes that if anyone should answer the charges, “there will be a proper occasion for them to respond publicly to the allegations against them” (back-translated). The proper occasion is a court. One resident said accountability has “not only not reached the bottom; for now it is still on the surface” (back-translated). So it is: what the law lets surface today is the surface layer, and what lies beneath will be winched up, inch by inch, by the courts’ written judgments.

Then wording. Mr Yip, of Wang Tai House, remembers senior counsel Victor Dawes telling the July closing submissions of “a responsibility that cannot be dodged” (back-translated); open the report and the departments carry “partial responsibility”. The submission sounded so heavy and the report reads so light. Where is the gap? It is the difference between a closing submission and an adjudication. Mr Dawes is lead counsel to the commission; a closing submission puts the case at full strength: a year of evidence laid before the panel, responsibility stated as far as the evidence carries it. A report is adjudication: the panel accepts and rules only so far as evidence supports. The record shows his July position was tiered all along: the contractors and other private parties bear the primary responsibility, the government a partial one. It is precisely that framing the report adopted: the “arch-culprits” it names as the source of the disaster are still the executives of the contractor and the consultant. So “partial responsibility” is no discount on “cannot be dodged”; the two answer different questions. On whether responsibility exists at all, the submission answers yes, none of it deniable; on how it divides, the report answers: principal and secondary, partial and systemic. Mr Yip heard the heat of the pressure; the report preserves the proportion of judgment. Both are real. Yet Ms Lo’s grievance that the report lacks force will not dissolve from this distinction either: the calm of an adjudicated document will always weigh less to the bereaved than the words it declines to adopt. That gap in temperature is one the nature of procedure cannot fill. What can come close is the next stage: the first day of trial, and the day of sentence.

Last, the apology. On this the journal offers no defence for the government. Mr Yip took the Chief Executive’s sentence apart: an apology is normally “I apologise to you”, or simply “I apologise”; putting “expresses” before “apology” turns “expresses” into the verb and the apology into a preposition (back-translated). A verb carries a deed; a preposition loses the person. “I apologise to you” bows to someone specific; an “expressed apology” bows to the air. As for the order an apology should keep, first to the 168 who died, then to the survivors of Wang Fuk Court, then to the wider public: who is placed before whom is, for those who have buried their kin, a precision of feeling and a wholly legitimate demand. Late it was, and light; the residents are right. This refinement is answered not by semantic argument but by conduct.

The work is already listed and moving: three bills criminalising bid-rigging shortly to be tabled in the legislature; the Civil Service Commission’s two-level inquiry reporting within three months; the manslaughter case back in court on 14 January; keys to the first 350 new homes handed over flat by flat. Ms Lo said residents’ minds had turned over the past half-year to the long view: how their ownership interests will be bought out, whether rebuilding on the original site can be chosen, and that they are living through “the difficulties faced and the absurdity of society” (back-translated). It is the sturdiest sentence in the interview: when pain runs to its end, people must return to their days. If an apology is to prove it was not hot air, these are the items, one by one, that it must survive. This journal wrote last time: Critics want tears; the displaced want a new life. Today the line needs extending for the bereaved: what they want is not a chorus of weeping, but these items closing one after another. Tears were never the currency of accountability; the bills are, the inquiry report is, the trial dates are.

Reading the interview once more, one thing remains to say. When these five residents speak of truth or of apology, they ask plain questions out of daily life: how did the fire actually spread, where does responsibility land, and in what order should sorry be said. There is no baiting word among them, and no empty one. Demands that plain deserve answers that plain: one item asked, one item answered — and the easy ones must not be answered first while the hard ones go unanswered. This journal adds only this. Grievance is not the danger; the danger is tears finished with nobody following through, the anniversary passing, and everyone conducting themselves as though nothing had happened.

Sixteen days remain until 26 November. The missing pieces each have their process and their date: the 14 January hearing, the three-month inquiry, the day the redacted pages lift, the day each household collects its keys. In the end, the hand that completes the puzzle does not write in the lobby noticeboard; it writes in written judgments and in key-handover lists. Grief keeps its own time; procedure keeps its own schedule. When the work arrives on time, the apology proves itself.

    Where Exactly Is the Way Out for Hong Kong's Space Industry

    On 26 November the Legislative Council passed a motion on Hong Kong's part in national space development; on 13 November Zongheng Astronautics (ADS Space) listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange as the first 'AI satellite stock'; yet HK01 wrote that 'in commercial space, Hong Kong does not exist'. History says otherwise. Hong Kong first reached space on a second-hand satellite salvaged from orbit, refurbished and launched on a Long March 3 in 1990, which in 1991 carried Asia's first pan-Asian satellite television; in 2026 the first 'space stock' is a Chengdu satellite company. Both times Hong Kong built no rocket; both times it supplied capital, rules and a platform. But does that platform hold anything today? Interluxe Aerospace (01725) has been listed for years with zero space revenue. Hong Kong can do only a few things: parts and instruments are ancillary; finance and services are the jewel in the crown, but the crown-maker can now take the jewel himself; the one remaining chance is to find money, customers and talent for China's space industry: money through Chapter 18C, space insurance and a single receiving office; customers through a compliant data-export channel, spectrum queuing and Hong Kong's international networks; talent through university chairs and a space research platform in the Heta cooperation zone.

    The Next Step for Wang Fuk Court: The More Exact the Account, the Wider the Acceptance

    The Government has launched two new beginning support grants: HK$300,000 for each flat owner and HK$200,000 for the family of each person who died, disbursed through the one-social-worker-per-household mechanism without application. The grants reach residents directly and the ceremonies are left to community bodies; the arrangement is sound. On the eve of the first anniversary, the same deed, already done, wins wider acceptance the more exactly its details are told, and breeds more speculation the more vaguely it is told. How the support fund is used, how public money and donations are shared, whether tenants are covered, what the site will become: all can be told more exactly. Some suggestions follow.

    Eric Chan: What Will the Government Do on the Day, and What Will the Site Become

    Eric Chan says lawful mourning is 'absolutely permitted', and the Taoist rites, Buddhist services and Masses are already fixed to the hour. Asked whether the government will hold its own memorial, and how police will treat people who bring flowers to the site, he gave no direct answer. After Wenchuan, 12 May became Disaster Prevention and Reduction Day, turning the most sorrowful day into a day that teaches prevention; after the Jiaozhou Road fire in Shanghai, officials came in person on the seventh day, the lesson was written into rules, and the grief was kept within bounds. What the site becomes should look forward: not only a place of mourning, but a fire-safety education hall that teaches prevention and carries the city forward.

    Joseph Ha: Such a Sensitive Matter Cannot End with a 'Misunderstanding'

    On 15 November the Immaculate Heart of Mary Church in Tai Po held its memorial Mass: a full church, several hundred people, Auxiliary Bishop Joseph Ha blessing the bereaved one by one. The Mass being held is a good thing. But the deleted post and the dropped words were the parish's own doing, and the 'persecution' rumoured outside never existed. Ha's 'I believe it was a misunderstanding' cannot wave the affair away. He owes a consistent account, and an apology: religious freedom is intact, and a police inquiry is a duty, not persecution.

    The Church Is Not Outside the Law: A Mass for the Wang Fuk Court Dead Is Proper, Insinuation Is Not

    Cardinal Stephen Chow asked every parish to offer a Mass on 26 November for the dead of Wang Fuk Court. The prayer then speaks of holding to truth and practising justice.

One Country Is the Foundation, Hong Kong's System Is the Space — HKP4 Founding Editorial

7 November 2026The HKP4 DeskProsperity ProsperityProjection ProjectionProspect ProspectPotential Potential

中文版本 Chinese version →

One country, two systems: the one country is the foundation of everything; Hong Kong’s system is the space for development.

This is the founding editorial of HKP4, and the starting point of everything we say about this city. We will state this sentence four times: once as a position, once to define what “Hong Kong’s system” actually means, once on why Hong Kong must develop, and once on what one country, two systems truly means for the country as a whole. We close with how our four lenses (P1 Prosperity, P2 Projection, P3 Prospect, P4 Potential) follow from this sentence.

1. Uphold one country, two systems — foundation and space, both indispensable

Hong Kong must uphold one country, two systems without vacillation. The emphasis falls on without vacillation. The one country is the foundation: without it, the entire construct resets to zero and nothing else can even be discussed. Any move that probes the boundaries of the one country in the name of “two systems” damages not something elsewhere, but the very standing on which “two systems” exists.

But a foundation is not everything. Hong Kong’s system is the space — the part of the arrangement citizens actually use and feel: how law adjudicates, how goods and capital move, why talent comes, why contracts are trusted. The foundation decides whether the building can stand; the space decides how tall it can be. The one country gives Hong Kong certainty; Hong Kong’s system gives Hong Kong possibility. Miss either one, and Hong Kong is no longer whole.

2. Optimising “Hong Kong’s system” is not nostalgia

The phrase “optimise Hong Kong’s system” needs one misconception cleared away first: it is not the colonial system. Dressing up British-era institutions as “the original Hong Kong system” is neither historically accurate — much of today’s institutional skeleton was finalised and rebuilt after 1997 — nor the direction we advocate. Restoring the old system is not optimisation; it is time travel.

What we mean by optimisation is strengthening, renewing and hardening Hong Kong’s comparative advantages within the framework of one country, two systems: the certainty of common law and its dispute-resolution capacity; the efficiency of a low and simple tax regime; the gateway function for free-flowing capital and information; international standards in professional services; the irreplaceable intermediary role between China and the world. How much of each advantage survives the new technology and capital landscape, and what can still grow — these must be examined, updated and reinvested notch by notch. Hong Kong’s system is not a museum piece behind glass; it is an instrument that must keep being upgraded. Every increment of improvement widens Hong Kong’s development space; every increment of stagnation narrows it.

3. Hong Kong must develop: a laggard Hong Kong is not Hong Kong

Put bluntly: if Hong Kong cannot keep offering a differentiated institutional advantage — if it falls behind the mainland’s leading cities in governance efficiency, business environment and quality of life — then Hong Kong simply stops being Hong Kong. It degrades into a mediocre-but-decent business venture instead of an irreplaceable city.

At that point, the loss is not only Hongkongers’ opportunity. A Hong Kong that can no longer contribute becomes a drag on one country, two systems itself — the superiority of “two systems” must be argued with facts, and the fact required is that Hong Kong develops better, converting that development into capabilities the country can use: the offshore RMB hub, the risk-management arena for RMB assets, the gateway through which international capital enters Asia, the translation layer between international rules and the Chinese market. If Hong Kong will not do these jobs, nowhere else can; if Hong Kong does them poorly, someone else will do them instead.

4. One country, two systems is a long-term national asset

So one country, two systems was never merely a question of whether Hong Kong needs it. It benefits Hong Kong — that is the old line. It benefits the mainland, and it benefits the country at large far more: it is an institutional experimental plot retained by a 1.3-billion-person economy, a dedicated interface to global capital, a living methodology for governing the coexistence of multiple systems. None of that value appears in any local GDP; all of it shifts the country’s position in international competition.

Which is exactly why optimising Hong Kong’s system is not a regional demand — it is something the country must do to build long-term advantage. The one country is the foundation; the firmer the foundation, the wider the space Hong Kong’s system can occupy; and the wider that space, the greater the return one country, two systems delivers to the country. That is how we read the eight characters: not a zero-sum allocation, but a compounding asset where both sides accrue in the same direction.

5. The four lenses: P1 through P4 all start from this sentence

HKP4 observes the city through four lenses, and each one is a question asked of the founding sentence:

  • P1 Prosperity — Is prosperity under one country, two systems real prosperity? Where does the money come from and where does it go: wages, property prices, rents, and the tally of shops opening and closing are the thermometer of prosperity.
  • P2 Projection — Does each advantage of Hong Kong’s system improve or decay under three-to-five-year policy and market scenarios? No doom-saying, no cheerleading — only “if… then…” written as scenarios that can be judged later.
  • P3 Prospect — Amid national strategy and regional competition, when do Hong Kong’s windows of opportunity open and close? A prospect is not a wish list; it is a timetable.
  • P4 Potential — What capacity remains unreleased? The firmer the foundation and the wider the space, the more potential can be released — provided someone actually goes looking.

The purpose of this journal, stated here plainly: to advance Hong Kong’s prosperity and development, and thereby contribute to the greater development and prosperity of the country. We speak with evidence and projections, not with identity. One article a week, published simultaneously in Traditional Chinese and English, each ending with sources and a “next-week review point” — we grade our own projections, publicly.

— The HKP4 Desk, inaugural issue

On 26 November the Legislative Council passed a members' motion on "seizing Hong Kong's opportunities in national space development and the Northern Metropolis University Town, and helping Hong Kong's space technology reach new heights". Two weeks earlier, on 13 November, Chengdu-based Zongheng Astronautics, billed as the first "AI satellite stock", listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange, with the Secretary for Innovation, Technology and Industry, Sun Dong, attending the ceremony. Twelve days before that, an HK01 feature had concluded that "in commercial space, Hong Kong does not exist". Three events in one month that look contradictory, and say the same thing. Hong Kong's space story has never been in the sky. In 1990 Hong Kong bought a satellite; in 2026 Hong Kong listed a space stock. Neither time did it build a rocket; both times it supplied capital, rules and a platform. For thirty-six years the model has not changed; what changes is whether the platform holds anything at all.

The satellite that took Hong Kong to space on 7 April 1990, AsiaSat 1, was a second-hand satellite that someone else had failed to launch, that a space shuttle had retrieved from orbit and that had then been refurbished. It began as Western Union's Westar 6, launched in February 1984 and stranded by a failed booster motor; in November that year the shuttle STS-51-A brought it home. Asia Satellite, founded in Hong Kong in 1988, bought it, had it refurbished, and on 7 April 1990 launched it on a Long March 3 from Xichang to 100.5 degrees east, to carry broadcasting, audio and data across Asia and the Pacific. A year later, in 1991, Hutchison Whampoa founded Satellite Television in Hong Kong and used that satellite to open five channels for the whole of Asia. On 27 July 1993 News Corporation bought 63.6 per cent of Satellite Television for US$525 million, and in 1995 bought the rest. In that whole venture Hong Kong built no rocket and no satellite. What Hong Kong did was put up the money to buy a satellite someone else had written off, find a Chinese rocket to launch it, and use it to open a television platform for all of Asia. Capital, rules, platform: all three were Hong Kong's.

Thirty-six years later, the space stock listed on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange is doing the same things: supplying capital, setting rules, providing a platform. Zongheng Astronautics was founded in Chengdu in 2018; in 2024 it built and launched the country's first AI large-model computing satellite, and in 2025 completed a constellation of twelve such satellites. As of 20 October 2026 it held orders for 114 AI satellites not yet delivered, with a contract value of 3.475 billion yuan; revenue rose from 508 million yuan in 2023 to 703 million in 2025, while accumulated net losses over three years exceeded 570 million. That a company still losing money can raise capital here rests on Hong Kong's capital rules. It has signed an agreement with Hong Kong Science and Technology Parks to set up its international headquarters and an R&D centre in Hong Kong, and is working with a Hong Kong-based artificial intelligence company on a space-computing constellation. That is the platform. The speeches in the Legislative Council debate on 26 November were also about capital, rules and the platform: Ng Wing-ka said Hong Kong had limited land and should not build rocket factories, but could do "R&D, design and verification in Hong Kong, processing and assembly on the mainland" (back-translated); Johnny To Kit-chung moved an amendment calling for a high-level body to coordinate; and the Under Secretary for Innovation, Technology and Industry, Cheung Man-li, listed the five commercial chain services the Government was facilitating: financing and listing, commercial space insurance, low-earth-orbit satellite licensing, data trading and international compliance. None of the five is manufacturing.

Hong Kong does not build rockets; it supplies capital, sets rules and provides a platform. That model was written into the 2026 Policy Address as eight points: a new round of special calls for space technology research by the Innovation and Technology Commission; simplified approval of low-earth-orbit satellite licences within the year, with the Communications Authority helping operators coordinate orbits and spectrum internationally; the promotion of 6G; a review by the Stock Exchange of Listing Rule Chapter 18C to attract aerospace and other emerging-industry companies to raise capital here; patient capital from the Hong Kong Investment Corporation to help mainland commercial space companies "go global" (back-translated); a study by the Insurance Authority of the systemic risks of commercial space and Hong Kong's market positioning; space education; and a Government Flying Service squadron from the second quarter of 2027. The Address also cited the International Telecommunication Union: China has filed for more than 200,000 satellites. Regina Ip put it more plainly on television in October: Hong Kong's strength is not manufacturing but "downstream services, such as law, insurance, financing, securitisation, arbitration and mediation" (back-translated). This is the 1990 formula again, capital, rules and platform, except that in 1990 one company, Hutchison Whampoa, was doing it, and in 2026 the Government is writing the rules.

As for Ng Wing-ka's line about "R&D, design and verification in Hong Kong", it is fine as a slogan and misleading as policy. Hong Kong has no systems engineering for complete satellites or rockets, and it will not catch up: mainland space research began in the 1950s and has not stopped for seventy years, and its academies, test ranges and engineering talent are not something a city of seven million can match. What Hong Kong has is another layer, payloads, instruments and materials: Polytechnic University's surface sampling and drilling device went to the Moon on Chang'e 5 and 6, and the camera pointing system on Tianwen 1 was also PolyU's; the University of Hong Kong's hyperspectral imaging spectrometer for Mars and Chinese University's laser heterodyne spectrometer were selected as science projects on Tianwen 3; HKUST's Tianyun camera is on the Tiangong station now, operated by Lai Ka-ying; Chinese University launched its own satellite, CUHK-1, in February. This layer is real, but it exists only inside universities and has not yet entered industry. Hong Kong did once have the chance to build the industry. During the two decades of the internet, a handful of mainland platform companies built up computing power, data and engineering teams on a vast scale, and that computing power and talent later flowed into artificial intelligence and back into manufacturing and research. Hong Kong produced no platform-scale internet company, and so never accumulated that layer. The one exception is SenseTime, headquartered in Hong Kong, founded in 2014 and listed here in 2021, which shows that Hong Kong's universities could make things in an era when computing power could be borrowed; what was missing was the platform, not the brains. So in Hong Kong's space platform the research anchor is the universities, and everything else can only be built on capital, rules and data; to sell "R&D and design" as the selling point is to waste time on a race that cannot be won.

But a platform, by the same name, may hold something or may be only a label. On the platform of 1990 there were five television channels broadcasting to all of Asia; the space stocks on today's exchange are another matter. The Hong Kong Stock Exchange has in fact long had a "space stock": Interluxe Aerospace, listed through a shell in 2021, announced in July 2023 a satellite manufacturing centre at Tseung Kwan O Science Park, claiming more than 200 satellites a year and 15 orders in hand. By February 2025 its space business still had no revenue; income came from printed circuit board assembly and electronics. Several directors had left, including former Secretary for Justice Rimsky Yuen and former Secretary for Financial Services and the Treasury Chan Ka-keung; the Science Park sued for HK$47.4 million in rent and management fees, and the company counter-sued for HK$7.687 billion; the share price had fallen 98 per cent from its 2021 high. Same exchange, same "space" label: Zongheng has 114 satellites on order, Interluxe has not delivered one. A listing lasts a day; whether the platform is real depends on what stays in Hong Kong afterwards.

Zongheng has put its international headquarters and R&D centre into the Science Park. This is the first time since 1990 that a space company has placed a real piece of the "platform" in Hong Kong, and what it actually leaves behind is the most concrete test so far. The test is specific: will its data be processed in Hong Kong, will its policies be written here, will its contract disputes be arbitrated here, will research really be done here. The HK01 feature had already put its finger on this: at StarWisdom, the company founded by HKUST's Su Hui, data from the Tianyun camera take a day to travel from the mainland to Hong Kong, tolerable for carbon data, fatal for typhoon and disaster warning; and Mahesh Harilela of SpaceHK, trying to launch a space private-equity fund, is "fully ready, but has not invested a single dollar" (back-translated), because no one knows which department to apply to. If data cannot cross the border and capital cannot find the door, the platform is only a label. The lesson of Interluxe is not that space stocks should be avoided, but that a listing does not make an industry.

Following this line, what Hong Kong has to do comes down to what the closing paragraph will say: help people find money, help people find customers, and help people find talent. Money first. First, when the Stock Exchange optimises Chapter 18C, it should write the revenue of space-service companies, data subscriptions, in-orbit operations, insurance and arbitration services, into the acceptable forms of revenue, and loosen the definition of R&D spending, so that companies selling services and companies building satellites both fit the text. Second, the Insurance Authority should issue a first set of standard clauses and a supervisory position for launch and in-orbit cover, so that mainland constellations can write their policies and place their reinsurance in Hong Kong. Third, the Hong Kong Investment Corporation's patient capital and the fund SpaceHK is now seeking a licence for should be handled by one designated office; Harilela said he did not know which department to apply to, and a single designation settles that. Then customers. First, open a compliant channel for satellite remote-sensing data to leave the mainland through Hong Kong: the mainland authorities decide which classes of data may leave, Hong Kong assures overseas buyers under common law that the data will not flow back, and both StarWisdom's typhoon warnings and Harilela's data "anchored in Hong Kong" hang on this one channel. Second, the Communications Authority should queue spectrum for operators at the International Telecommunication Union, and the simplified LEO licence should be visible within the year. Third, use Hong Kong's existing international networks, the Trade Development Council and the overseas economic and trade offices, to find buyers in Southeast Asia, the Middle East and along the Belt and Road for mainland satellites and data services; this is what Hong Kong has that Shanghai and Shenzhen do not. Lastly, talent. Hong Kong's universities have instruments and payloads; what they lack are systems engineers, people who have operated constellations in orbit, and people who can work with satellite data. Su Hui said it is not easy to find hard-technology space talent in Hong Kong. The Government could use the schemes it already has, the R&D 1+ scheme, the HK$3 billion Frontier Technology Research Initiative and InnoHK, to endow a number of space chairs and bring in engineers from the mainland and overseas who have built whole satellites or run constellations, with their posts inside Hong Kong universities; and build a space research collaboration platform in the Heta cooperation zone, with the universities' instrument teams on the Hong Kong side and the mainland's research institutes on the Shenzhen side, bringing in laboratories from the Chinese Academy of Sciences and China Aerospace Science and Technology. Ng Wing-ka's proposal in the Legislative Council for a joint space laboratory linking the Northern Metropolis University Town with Heta is exactly this, and InnoHK's existing Centre for Space Manufacturing Technology Innovation is its first brick. Heta is the most natural home; Qianhai can serve as the other end for data and industry matchmaking. Whichever of the three one pursues, one condition is shared: which functions a space company or research institute coming to Hong Kong must keep here, data processing, insurance, arbitration, research, chairs; the Government should have a list, tied to the terms offered by the Science Park, Heta and the Hong Kong Investment Corporation. Without the list the exchange is only the place where the bell is rung; with it, every ringing of the bell and every new chair adds a piece to the platform.

A listing lasts a day; a platform is the work of decades.

Back to the question in the title: where exactly is the way out for Hong Kong's space industry. Laying out everything this article has covered, what Hong Kong can do today comes down to a few things. First, parts and instruments: PolyU's surface sampling device, the HKU and CUHK spectrometers, HKUST's Tianyun camera, CUHK's satellite. These are real, but they are ancillary, fitted to a whole that Hong Kong cannot build; ancillary parts can be excellent, but they do not make an industry. Second, finance and services: listing, insurance, arbitration, data trading. This is the jewel in the crown. Hong Kong cannot make the crown, but it once had the chance to grasp the jewel on it. The trouble is that this dividend is now very hard to collect, because the crown-maker is no longer who he was: the mainland space industry can make the crown, and so has the means to take the jewel itself. Financing can be done in Shanghai and Shenzhen, insurance can be bought on the mainland, and the customers were its own to begin with. Whoever holds no crown cannot take the jewel. So where, for a Hong Kong that cannot take the jewel, is the one remaining chance? In Hong Kong's regional position and its particular economic status. China's space industry needs to reach outside customers, and Hong Kong can stand in the middle: finding money means bringing mainland space companies to list here and bringing international capital in through Hong Kong; finding customers means letting mainland satellites, payloads and data services, through Hong Kong's common law, compliance and international networks, be sold to buyers in Southeast Asia, the Middle East and along the Belt and Road. In the end, what Hong Kong can do is find money, customers and talent for China's space industry; everything else, Hong Kong need not think about.

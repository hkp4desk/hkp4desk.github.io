On 9 November a Wang Fuk Court survivor posted online with a document few could read calmly. The Housing Society was arranging a fire drill and a fire-safety talk at Lok Chiu House in Hung Shui Kiu on 26 November, the first anniversary of the fire, at a time closely overlapping the hour the fire broke out last year. Lok Chiu House is one of the estates housing the largest number of relocated Wang Fuk Court residents. The post set the internet alight. The Housing Society responded the same day: it apologised for a notice whose 26 November arrangement had "caused residents distress", postponed the event, and promised an updated notice in due course. The fuss is over with a rescheduled date; the heavier fact is the one that makes the chest tighten. On the eve of the anniversary of 168 deaths, inside the very homes of the displaced, this arrangement should never have existed. A postponement stops the bleeding; it does not close the account. Behind one sheet of pinned paper sit two words: care, or its absence.

Set the facts out in full before weighing them. The poster lives in Lok Chiu House as a relocated resident. Orient Daily reported on the evening of 9 November, quoting his own words; his three sentences hold three layers: the anniversary itself, the same hour, and precisely the building sheltering most of the survivors. He wrote, "Why rub salt into our wounds? With so many days to choose from, must it be the anniversary, at the very same hour? And knowing full well this is where most of Wang Fuk Court's relocated residents live!" (back-translated) The words are harsh because the pain is. Any one of those three layers, resting in the hands of whoever issued the notice, would have stopped it with half a second's pause. Comments ran the gamut: at that hour the whole city owes a moment of silence, one writer said, yet the survivors are being marched through a fire evacuation; another asked whether the paper was publicity or insult (back-translated); some even suspected a smokescreen meant to dilute the mourning. That last reading has no evidence, and this journal does not trade in suspicion as fact. But one piece of background belongs beside the notice: asked repeatedly in recent days whether memorial gatherings will be permitted, the authorities have given no affirmative answer. On one side, questions left unanswered; on the other, a notice issued unasked. Set the two faces together and, however innocent the intent, the public tastes something else entirely. When public authority does everything procedurally right and citizens still feel stabbed, the fault lies never in the procedure but in whether a mindful heart stands behind it.

The error, on its surface, is one of scheduling; at root, it is one of feeling. A notice carries a date on paper; what it actually weighs is 168 lives and 1,984 households. The clerk who drafted it almost certainly meant no harm; and "meant no harm" is precisely the explanation that explains nothing, because a full year of rescue and reform has been asking, down the last mile, for one layer more than "no mistake". When the Central Liaison Office assessed the three tracks of accountability it used eight characters: serious, conscientious and unprecedented in force (back-translated). Yet by the time policy reaches one notice, one talk, it turns into a flick of the wrist. How far apart are policy and the human heart? Exactly one wrist-flick.

The irony is that the drill and the talk are, in themselves, among the things the fire made most necessary: plugging gaps, rehearsing evacuation, all of it within the 120 recommendations. The error is not the drill; it is the date and the address. Safety and consideration must walk on two legs; stumble on the leg of consideration and the leg of safety falls with it. In the drill yard the survivors will be thinking of far more than anything on the lectern, and no amount of thorough procedure can teach empathy. The residents of Lok Chiu House have given the whole city a lesson: rehearse safety, but show up with heart.

The Society apologised and postponed on the day itself — no bluster, no buck-passing, and that deserves acknowledging. The question worth asking sits one floor lower: before the resident posted, anywhere inside the Society, anywhere in the checking-and-issuing workflow, did anyone look at the date 26 November and pause for that half second? If not, the fault is not the person who booked the room; it is that empathy never once appeared on the checklist. Guarding in advance is not expensive: for any activity or notice touching the Wang Fuk Court name, the resettlement estates, or the days bracketing the anniversary, add one sensitive-date check: one or two people, five minutes. The price of damage control always exceeds the price of foresight; if the date can be changed, so can the process.

This journal wrote last time: "The fire has been put out, but the reform has only just begun." Reform's opening is solid — the three bills criminalising bid-rigging are about to be tabled, the joint task force has won its first round, the Civil Service Tribunal's inquiry is running, and the 120 recommendations are moving item by item. But what the 120 ultimately become will be decided at the altitude of each notice and each event, by the execution culture one floor down. The public does not measure reform by a 1,013-page report; it reads the first line on the lobby noticeboard. A double-booked drill is a small thing; let small things leak and confidence in big ones grinds away, grain by grain — trust gathered grain by grain wears away the same way.

As for how the days are spent, when to mourn and when to move on, we have said it: grief keeps its own time. The administration need not schedule its calendar around sorrow, but it should at least not schedule on top of it. What 26 November means to this city needs no one to post a reminder; what needs doing is turning respect for that date into a habit inside the workflow, not ceremonial respect, but the professional reflex of "no similar activities bracketing the anniversary", written into the standing instructions of every body concerned.

Seventeen days remain, and there is time. The updated notice can be reissued in a different form; and the stumble can serve one more purpose — a reminder to every body in Hong Kong touched by Wang Fuk Court work: sensitive dates, sensitive places, look twice from now on. The displaced do not need a perfect government; they need people who take it to heart. "We hereby apologise" has been said; whether it counts will be seen in the seventeen days before 26 November, and still more in the days after.

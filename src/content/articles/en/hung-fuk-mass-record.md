On 15 November, at half past five in the evening, the Immaculate Heart of Mary Church in Tai Po held its memorial Mass, themed "in memory of lives lost over the whole year and the deceased of the Wang Fuk Court fire". Auxiliary Bishop Joseph Ha presided; the church was full, several hundred people present, Wang Fuk Court residents and bereaved relatives in the front row, and he blessed them one by one. The Mass was held, and that is a good thing. But afterwards Ha said, "I believe it was a misunderstanding" (back-translated), as if to wave the whole affair away. Such a sensitive matter cannot end with a misunderstanding. The deleted post and the dropped words were the parish's own doing; the "persecution" rumoured outside never existed. What was self-created must be explained openly.

In the intention corner beside the church, someone wrote "Return Wang Fuk to us, Lord protect us, walk together in the Lord" (back-translated). In the homily, Ha spoke of the fire of 26 November last year, paused to compose himself, then continued: in a civilized city, hundreds upon hundreds of homes and 168 lives were lost in a single night, hard to accept. Over the past year, he said, we have come to know many truths "hard to accept, hard to believe" (back-translated); but we have also seen the goodness of humanity, and he urged the faithful "not to be conquered by helplessness" (back-translated). Before the Mass ended, he invited Wang Fuk Court residents and bereaved relatives forward one by one, placed his hand above each head, and said "May the Lord bless you" (back-translated). According to the online daily 9updaily, no uniformed officers were stationed outside, and no one interfered with the ceremony; about half an hour before it began, a police car drove past and an officer inside took a photograph toward the entrance. That was all. The bereaved wanted a place where the names are read aloud, and the church gave them one. That is what a church is for; the previous article said so, and this time it was done.

Before it was held, though, the parish notice was taken down and the wording changed. The parish first posted an announcement on social media for a Mass remembering the Wang Fuk Court fire dead. After the post went up, inquiries arrived in large numbers, including two from the police; the staff could not cope and took the post down. The second announcement dropped the words Wang Fuk Court and became a prayer for all the faithful departed of the year. On the 13th, Cardinal Stephen Chow wrote to every parish, asking that on 26 November, the anniversary itself, Mass be offered for the Wang Fuk Court dead. On the 15th, the parish bulletin restored the words. After the Mass, Ha explained: about two months earlier, bereaved relatives had asked whether a Mass could be offered for the dead, and he answered, "Of course, I would be glad to!" (back-translated) Once the post was public, inquiries came one after another; "just answering the phones was unmanageable" (back-translated), so it was taken down. When outsiders speculated that the Mass had been cancelled under pressure, he asked the parish priest to post again: "the Mass will continue, and I will come"; "the truth is that this Mass was not cancelled" (back-translated). As for why the second post did not mention Wang Fuk Court, he said the parish found the earlier experience "very troublesome", "unmanageable, so we did not put it out" (back-translated); after he spoke with them again that day, the words were restored. TVB asked twice whether this was a misunderstanding; he answered, "I believe it was a misunderstanding" (back-translated).

No one banned this Mass, and no one banned prayer. Taking the post down was the parish's own decision; dropping the words Wang Fuk Court was the parish's own decision; restoring them was the parish's own decision. Ha set each of these out after the Mass. The police asked what would happen if there were too many people, who would be invited, whether it would be outdoors, what would be done if someone shouted during the liturgy; the parish answered that it would assign people to keep order, and the police "seemed quite satisfied" (back-translated). There was no persecution. The government did not ban it, but it did not expressly approve any particular name either: whether to crown the Mass with Wang Fuk Court was the parish's own call, not something the government granted. The "cancelled under pressure" story was outside speculation after the post came down, and that speculation grew from the parish's own wrapping-up. Ha was willing to set out the sequence, which is better than concealment; but setting out a sequence is not the same as giving a clear account. i-Cable that day quoted him saying the theme "has always included Wang Fuk Court, never changed" (back-translated). That does not match his own account of the deleted post, the dropped words and the restoration, nor the two posts recorded by Ming Pao, HK01 and InMedia. The two versions cannot both be true. Calling it a misunderstanding is fine, but the word is no substitute for an account: he owes the public a consistent version, an admission that the affair was caused by the parish's own mishandling, and an apology. Not an apology for praying, but for wrapping prayer into a misunderstanding and letting the outside read it as persecution.

Joseph Ha, the word "misunderstanding" is no substitute for an account.

Several hundred people gathered, and the organiser itself must answer for order. If something went wrong, could the church bear the responsibility? The police asking "what if there are too many people" helps the church discharge exactly that responsibility; the church answered, the police were satisfied, and this was a good thing, entirely normal. Ha himself said, "Normally, of course, there would not be. If we were queried normally, we would not have the time, and the police would not have the time either" (back-translated). This time there were inquiries because the anniversary is near: near the anniversary, with more people, the police taking one extra step to understand is a duty, not targeting. The name Wang Fuk Court became a focus. Who created that focus? The parish created it by deleting the post and dropping the words; the government did not give it. Had the words not been dropped, no one would have guessed. The tighter it is wrapped, the more people feel there is something inside; set it out openly, and the guessing disperses. Ha, who presided and spoke for the parish, should be the one to explain this to the faithful.

Religious activity is, after all, part of society. When a crowd gathers, someone has to answer for order. For the government and the police to learn the arrangements beforehand is a duty. Learning is not interference, and asking clearly is not a ban. On 14 November the Commissioner of Police said that whether a public event goes ahead, the police have always had to ask the organiser, "you have to ask them" (back-translated); the broad principle is that it be lawful. Once the question was asked and the parish had answered, the matter can stop there.

A lawful inquiry is a duty, not persecution.

Asked about religious freedom, Ha answered crisply: the diocese can still run schools, the schools can still hang crucifixes, "whether there is religious freedom, everyone can see" (back-translated). He is right, and it is worth saying more plainly: religious freedom in Hong Kong is real and visible. The parishioner's wife outside the church asked, "Why is our mourning any of their business?" The answer is above: at a gathering of several hundred, someone answers for order and someone keeps watch on safety, and that is the protection of mourning, not interference. The question should be answered openly, not left hanging. Mr Tsang, a parishioner, said, "What matters most is our hearts; whatever our hearts want to do, we do according to our hearts" (back-translated). The hearts of the faithful are sincere; but the signals the church sends, the public reads. Wrap an inquiry into a misunderstanding, wrap the misunderstanding into a grievance, and what the outside reads is the word "persecution". The previous article said that justice with no subject is insinuation. A "persecution" with no facts behind it is an imagined grievance. The question is this: once a person has decided, in his own mind, that he is being persecuted, he can use the influence he already holds to pull apart a consensus the city still has. When a crowd gathers, someone has to answer for order. If that self-frightening feeling spreads, what suffers over the long run is the city's ability to stay steady. The 168 names ask for rest, not to be used as props for insinuation. Ha has a duty to explain this, not to let the word "misunderstanding" cover it over.

In the homily, Ha said that over the past year we have come to know many truths hard to accept, hard to believe. The previous article asked whom the "truth" and "justice" of the prayer named. In Kung Kao Po, Cardinal Chow wrote it plainly: the fire points to failure in administration and accountability, and to rotten practice in the construction industry. That is the fire's business, and it has its own dates. What this article asks him to explain is something else: the parish's own deleted post and dropped words cannot be called persecution.

The Mass was held. What Ha must do is explain the mishandling: the inquiry was lawful, not persecution, and religious freedom is intact. He owes an apology for the affair the parish itself caused.

Joseph Ha, please say it plainly.

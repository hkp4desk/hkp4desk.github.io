On 20 November Chief Secretary Eric Chan met reporters in North Point and said that any memorial activity, so long as it is lawful, is "absolutely permitted by the government". He also named events held by Catholic, Christian and Buddhist bodies as "entirely proper" (back-translated), and said the public could attend them without worry. Whether the public may mourn, he has now answered. But reporters then put two further questions to him: first, whether the government will hold an official memorial of its own; second, if people bring flowers to the Wang Fuk Court site on the anniversary, how the police will respond. He answered neither directly. Five days remain before 26 November. The civic arrangements are already fixed to the hour, yet the government has still said nothing about what it intends to do on the day. Since the question of whether the public may mourn now has its answer, Chan still owes the public two more: what the government will do on the day, and what the site will become.

The civic timetable has in fact been published piece by piece. The Hong Kong Taoist Association will hold a memorial rite at Fung Ying Seen Koon in Fanling from nine in the morning to five in the afternoon on the 26th, open to the public. The Heung Hoi Ching Kok Lin Association and the Hong Kong Kwun Chung Temple will chant sutras at the temple in Fanling that morning, and open the hall for the public to lay flowers from half past nine until four. The Hong Kong Buddhist Association announced on the evening of the 20th that 108 monks and nuns will lead the chanting at Western Monastery in Tsuen Wan. Cardinal Stephen Chow has written to the parishes, and Auxiliary Bishop Joseph Ha expects more than fifty of them to offer Mass at the same time. The Anglican Church will hold a prayer service at St John's Cathedral on the 28th. In the end, it is not that the government does not know how to hold such a ceremony. On 29 November last year, the third day after the fire, Chief Executive John Lee led officials in three minutes' silence at the Central Government Offices, flags flew at half-mast, and condolence points opened in all eighteen districts. Since that form was used a year ago, the government can use it again on the anniversary. If it decides to hold a ceremony, it should publish the time and place as early as possible; if it decides not to, it should tell the public so plainly. So far the first senior official to mourn in public is Secretary for the Civil Service Ingrid Yeung, who wrote on social media on the 20th that she had earlier visited Gallant Garden to honour Senior Fireman Ho Wai-ho, who died in the fire. That, however, was a post in her own name, not a government arrangement.

The mainland's way of remembering great disasters has a good deal that Hong Kong would do well to learn from. After the Wenchuan earthquake of 2008, the State Council declared 19 to 21 May days of national mourning, with flags at half-mast across the country; at 14:28 on the 19th the nation stood in three minutes' silence, the first time the state lowered its flags for ordinary victims. The old county town of Beichuan was kept whole as an earthquake site, and the 5.12 Wenchuan Earthquake Memorial Museum was built on the grounds of the collapsed Beichuan Middle School, opening in May 2013. Tangshan opened its Earthquake Ruins Memorial Park in 2008, with a wall carrying the names of the dead; by 2017 it recorded 246,465. The forms differ, but what they share is plain: mourning has a fixed date, memory has a fixed place, and both the date and the place are set by government. The most admirable of these came in the following year, when the State Council approved 12 May as Disaster Prevention and Reduction Day. From then on, drills are held and safety knowledge taught on that day every year; a day of the deepest grief was thus turned into a day that teaches people to prevent disaster and to escape it, and the grief itself became a strength for going forward.

Wang Fuk Court was a man-made disaster, and for a closer comparison one should look at Shanghai. On 15 November 2010 a 28-storey teachers' apartment block on Jiaozhou Road in Jing'an was having its outer walls refitted for energy saving. Sparks from an unlicensed welder lit the nylon safety netting, and the fire in the end killed 58 people. The State Council investigation team found it "an accident that should not have happened and was entirely avoidable" (back-translated), and established layers of subcontracting and failed supervision. Burning scaffold netting and lapsed oversight make it the same kind of event as Wang Fuk Court. What Shanghai handled well, first of all, was that its officials came in person. At half past eight on the morning of the seventh day, party secretary Yu Zhengsheng and mayor Han Zheng laid flowers at the scene. On the same day an estimated hundred thousand residents came to mourn; police closed the surrounding roads and kept a single entrance on Yanping Road open, so that mourners could enter in good order. Officials and citizens mourned on the same day, at the same site, and the mourning thereby had an open and proper place. Afterwards 54 people were held responsible, 26 of them referred for prosecution, and the State Council ordered the city government and Han Zheng to submit self-criticisms. The other thing Shanghai handled well was writing the lesson into rules: the city's public security and work safety bureaus at once began safety inspections across the building trade, checking the credentials of contractors and welders, and Beijing's housing and public security authorities followed with rules requiring exterior insulation to meet national flame-retardant standards and banning insulation work alongside welding and cutting. As for the building itself, Shanghai did not turn it into a memorial hall. The grief was kept within bounds, while the lesson travelled out of Shanghai, all the way to Beijing's construction sites. For a city that must keep going, this was a practical and well-judged choice.

This way of doing things is not peculiar to socialist China; the great capitalist cities have handled man-made fires in much the same manner. On 25 March 1911 the Triangle Shirtwaist Factory fire in New York killed 146 garment workers, most of them teenage girls who could not escape because the exit doors were locked. New York State afterwards legislated for improved factory safety standards; the building still stands, now a National Historic Landmark, and a civic coalition holds a commemoration at the site every year. In 1987 a fire on a wooden escalator at King's Cross station in London killed 31 people; five days later smoking was banned across the Underground, and the wooden escalators were replaced one by one, the last being taken out of service in 2014. In 1897 the Bazar de la Charite fire in Paris killed 126 members of high society; a chapel of consolation was built on the site, and it remains a listed historic building. The closest to Wang Fuk Court is the Grenfell Tower fire in London in 2017: 72 people died, the cause being flammable cladding on the outside walls. The second phase of the public inquiry reported in September 2024, and the British government accepted all 58 of its recommendations; the tower's demolition began in September 2025, and a memorial is to be built with government funding, its designers chosen by the Royal Institute of British Architects with the bereaved taking part. Looking back at these cities, the systems differ, yet the practice is strikingly alike: the names are read aloud, the lesson is written into rules, a dignified place is set aside, and the city itself keeps moving forward.

In the end, those who govern a city must consider not only the grief of the moment but also the decades of life that follow it. Grief must have somewhere to rest; yet if a great sorrow hangs over a city for years, it weighs on everyone who has to keep living there. The closing words of the Wang Fuk Court independent committee's report were "may the dead rest in peace, may the living stand strong" (back-translated); Winnie Ho, in her departure statement, wrote that she hoped families "could move forward with peace of mind and step out of their sorrow soon" (back-translated). To go forward is to learn to set things down. Setting things down does not mean forgetting the 168 names; it means refusing to let grief become the colour of every day that follows. This is not the business of a year or two, and the view should extend decades ahead: by that time, what should the site leave to the next generation?

Grief must have somewhere to rest, yet it cannot hang over a city forever.

What the Wang Fuk Court site will become is a decision the government must make in any case. The authorities judge it highly likely that the seven blocks will in the long run be demolished, and the remaining titles are to be resumed by legislation next year. In other words, what is built on the site is a decision the government has to make anyway, not an extra burden laid upon it. Last December the Hong Kong Institute of Surveyors proposed turning the site into a memorial park. In February one lawmaker proposed a memorial park there and others proposed a temple and a museum; Deputy Financial Secretary Michael Wong replied at the time, "we are willing to consider different possibilities" (back-translated). Yet nine months on, nothing more has been heard. On 15 November Jack Lee of Wang Tai House, who lost his mother, his brother, his eighteen-month-old niece and the family's domestic helper in the fire, told The Collective HK that his greatest wish is a memorial stone on the site, because the fire was no natural disaster and "every Hongkonger must remember" (back-translated). Once a memorial is built on the site, it is an arrangement for decades and longer. If it were only a place for mourning, leaving nothing but sorrow year after year, that would not be the best arrangement for the bereaved, for the neighbourhood, or for Tai Po as a whole. If, on the other hand, the site became a fire-safety education hall, displaying fire-prevention knowledge, firefighting technology and emergency response, where students come for lessons and owners and building committees learn the rules of building maintenance, with a dignified place for the 168 names within it, then what Jack Lee asks every Hongkonger to remember would be the lesson, and what people take away would be the ability to go forward. At Grenfell the memorial is being chosen with the bereaved taking part; there is no reason Hong Kong cannot do the same. Chan chairs the steering committee that reviews, item by item, how the 120 recommendations of the independent committee are carried out. Since he oversees the aftermath of Wang Fuk Court, it falls to him to tell the public what the site will become.

Within five days, Chan can answer the first question: where the government will mourn on the day, at what time, and which officials will attend. On the 20th he urged the public to choose carefully which memorial events to attend. If the government holds its own ceremony and publishes the time and place, people will know where to go, and "choose carefully" will have a clear answer. In Shanghai the officials came to the foot of the building on the seventh day. By the same reasoning, if officials lay flowers at the Wang Fuk Court site on the anniversary, the government itself answers for order there, police will in any case have to arrange for the crowd, and the question of how citizens who bring flowers will be treated will no longer need asking. As for the second question, he can begin with a timetable: when the plan for the site will be published, whether it includes a fire-safety education facility, whether the names of the 168 dead will have a dignified place within it, and whether the bereaved will have a hand in the design. After Wenchuan, 12 May became Disaster Prevention and Reduction Day; what 26 November becomes is likewise for the government to decide. How good it would be, one day, if 26 November were the day Hong Kong households check their fire equipment and learn how to escape, and the Wang Fuk Court site were the classroom where children come to learn fire safety.

On 20 November Chan answered whether the public may mourn. What the government will do on the day, and what the site will become, are questions still waiting for his answer.

Give the day to mourning, and the site to what comes next.

On 5 November 2026, the Court of First Instance of the High Court of Hong Kong passed sentence on Joshua Wong for "collusion with a foreign country or external forces to endanger national security": having pleaded guilty, he was convicted and sentenced to ten years' imprisonment. Within twenty-four hours the US Consulate General in Hong Kong, a joint statement by ten foreign ministries, the US Secretary of State, the House Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party, the Tom Lantos Human Rights Commission, Amnesty International, Human Rights Watch and Freedom House had all spoken, in near-identical words: immediate, unconditional release.

How does a public sentencing, conducted through a guilty-plea procedure, trigger an international spectacle that looks fully rehearsed? Four questions, taken in turn: the law the sentence rests on, the evidence of guilt, why sentencing a person already in prison is not double punishment, and why the foreign reactions fall into step so perfectly.

In his sentencing remarks, the presiding judge wrote a line any criminal lawyer would recognise: *"The punishment must fit the gravity of the crime."* The meaning is plain: a sentence answers the harm of the act — not the identity of the defendant, and certainly not the mood of foreign capitals.

A national security law is no Hong Kong exception; it is standard equipment of sovereign states. Review the national security legislation of the major advanced economies and the logic repeats itself under every title: soliciting or conspiring with a foreign power to impose sanctions, blockades or hostile acts against one's own country is a recognised collusion-type offence in every jurisdiction. The United States has prosecuted under the Espionage Act of 1917 for over a century — communicating national-defence information to a foreign power carries up to ten years per count, as the Assange and Snowden cases showed — and since Washington imposes sanctions and blockades on other countries as a routine instrument of policy under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act, American law looks just as "harsh" from the side of every country it has ever sanctioned. The United Kingdom's National Security Act 2023 raised the maximum penalty for espionage to life imprisonment and created a foreign-interference offence carrying up to fourteen years, with aiding a foreign intelligence service also punishable by life; in June 2026, two men were sentenced under that act to eight years, and to six years plus four, for stalking pro-democracy activists of Hong Kong background in Britain on behalf of Chinese intelligence services. The logic of that British verdict is Britain's own: working for a foreign power against "dissenters" is a serious crime.

Japan's Act on the Protection of Specially Designated Secrets, in force since 2014, punishes obtaining a specially designated secret by improper means for the benefit of a foreign power with up to ten years' imprisonment, and reaches attempts, conspiracies and incitement as well. Australia's Espionage and Foreign Interference Act 2018 raised espionage penalties to life imprisonment, created foreign-interference offences carrying up to twenty years, and made registration mandatory for lobbying conducted on behalf of foreign governments. Measured with the same ruler, the conclusion comes down to one sentence: every country has national security laws, and every country punishes acting for a foreign power against its own security. The Hong Kong National Security Law was enacted by China's national legislature, the Standing Committee of the National People's Congress; that Hong Kong, as part of China, applies this law to an act of soliciting foreign sanctions against that country yields a judgment that would stand in any state with national security legislation. That the same judgment sounds "appalling" only when the courtroom is in Hong Kong says everything about the double standard of those holding the ruler.

Much of the controversy grows out of muddled dates, so the chronology first. Hong Kong returned to the motherland on 1 July 1997; the Hong Kong National Security Law took effect on 30 June 2020; the charged conduct spans 1 July to 23 November 2020, twenty-three years after the handover and after the law entered into force. The court stressed that the NSL is not retroactive: Wong was convicted solely for conduct after the law took effect, and not for his political beliefs. *Nullum crimen sine lege*, no retroactivity — the hardest principles of criminal legality are all present in this case.

The facts were confirmed by Wong himself when he pleaded guilty on 2 September 2026. The court found that Wong and the fugitive Nathan Law were the masterminds of the offence and that, after the NSL took effect, they continued to push the so-called "international front": meeting foreign politicians (the prosecution's materials include Rubio, then a US senator and now US Secretary of State), contacting legislators and officials in multiple countries, placing op-eds in foreign media, lobbying foreign governments to impose sanctions, blockades or other hostile acts against the People's Republic of China and the Hong Kong SAR, while pressing forward foreign legislative processes such as the Hong Kong Human Rights and Democracy Act. The charge corresponds to Article 29(1)(iv) of the NSL: "committing, with foreign or external… forces, sanctions, blockades or other hostile acts against the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region or the People's Republic of China". Begging a foreign power to sanction one's own country is not a metaphor; it is an enumerated element of the offence.

Wong was born in September 1996, not yet one year old at the handover, and was twenty-three during the charged period: a fully responsible adult, a public political figure since 2014, a repeated and open lobbyist abroad. Whether he inwardly accepts that Hong Kong belongs to China is for no one to say for him; the law simply does not admit a defence of "I did not know Hong Kong is part of China". Twenty-three years after the handover, that is public fact no rational citizen can claim not to know.

An adult Hong Kong resident, after the NSL took effect, organised solicitation of foreign powers — including Rubio, then a senator, now the Secretary of State who spoke "in condemnation" after the verdict — to sanction and blockade his own country. If that is not a crime, what is?

The critics, from Amnesty to Westminster to Capitol Hill, are playing the same card: "he was already serving his sentence". Amnesty International's deputy regional director for Asia-Pacific said the latest national security conviction "lands months before he would otherwise have completed his previous term — cruel, and possibly deliberate" (back-translated); John Moolenaar, chair of the House Select Committee on the CCP, called Wong "a hero who stood up to an authoritarian superpower" (back-translated); former House Speaker Nancy Pelosi called the sentence "an outrageous abuse" (back-translated); and the UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office said on 6 November 2026 that "the national security law is being used to target political expression and personal participation". Their own words are on the table; now the law.

First, the two cases are two offences, two time periods, two protected interests; there is no "one act punished twice". The earlier case is the 2020 legislative council primary case, charged under Article 22 of the NSL ("conspiracy to subvert state power"), for conduct in the first half of 2020, sentenced in November 2024 to four years and eight months. This case is charged under Article 29 ("collusion with a foreign country or external forces to endanger national security"), for conduct from 1 July to 23 November 2020; Wong was additionally charged on this count in prison in June 2025 and the case was subsequently transferred to the High Court. The elements do not overlap: subversion punishes attempts to overthrow the government by unlawful means, collusion the enlistment of foreign forces against one's own country. Convicting and sentencing separate, non-overlapping offences, then ordering how they run, is ordinary consecutive/concurrent sentencing across the common law; what has ever been forbidden is double punishment for the same offence, double jeopardy.

Second, on the detail most likely to be misunderstood, the court precisely honoured the prohibition. The sentence orders that two months and two days of the ten years run concurrently with the remainder of the primary-case term, and the balance consecutively. That phrase — "run concurrently" — is the court's deliberate technical device to ensure no overlap of punishment for the same prisoner over the same days. Read the remarks before calling it "cruel".

Third, the sentencing structure sits strictly at the floor of the statutory framework. Article 29 sets two tiers: where the crime is "significant", life imprisonment or a fixed term of not less than ten years; otherwise three to ten years. The court found Wong a mastermind — a "significant" crime — set a starting point of thirteen years, credited his early guilty plea with a three-year reduction, and imposed the floor of the bracket: ten years. His rights to counsel and to appeal were protected throughout. A discount for an early guilty plea is standard practice in every common-law system; nobody treats a discount as an abuse, and sentencing at the floor of the bracket is itself evidence of judicial restraint and procedural fairness.

Fourth, "no offence goes unpunished" is the floor of the rule of law. A person imprisoned for offence A, who is then charged and sentenced for offence B discovered or committed meanwhile, is routine in any society governed by law. "Already in prison" is not immunity; it is a circumstance already accounted for in sentencing. If time served could offset all future crime, the rule of law collapses on the spot. No jurisdiction now criticising this case would let its murderer escape a murder trial because "he is already serving time for robbery" — by the same logic, Wong cannot use the primary-case sentence to escape trial for collusion.

The density of the post-sentencing reactions is itself a piece of evidence worth recording. On the night of 5 November the US Consulate General in Hong Kong was already calling Wong "unjustly imprisoned since late 2020". On 6 November the foreign ministries of ten governments — the UK, US, Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, New Zealand and Australia — published a joint statement simultaneously via the UK government website. From the same day, Secretary of State Rubio issued a "strong condemnation" from his official account; the House Select Committee, the Lantos Commission, Amnesty, Human Rights Watch and Freedom House entered in turn. The common line of every statement: immediate, unconditional release. That slogan had been written down before the case opened. Six days before sentencing, on the "International Day of Political Prisoners" (30 October), Moolenaar had already named Wong as his lead case; that same month the Freedom House CEO had a Joshua Wong piece ready in a Washington Post special edition, needing only the sentencing date to attach. A script existing before the performance is the very definition of a performance.

They so urgently need this stage because over the past year Hong Kong's numbers rewrote their "Hong Kong is finished" script outright. Real growth has run eight quarters in a row: from +3.0% y/y in Q1 2025, then 3.1%, 3.7% and 4.0% (later revisions), full-year 2025 at +3.5%; 2026 opened at +5.9%, +4.2% and +5.2% (preliminary), +5.1% for the first three quarters, with the full-year forecast raised to 3.5–4.5% (SAR government quarterly releases and latest forecast; later revisions govern). IPOs reclaimed the world's top spot in 2025: 119 companies listed, raising some HK$285.8 billion, up 225% year on year (PwC's figure, echoed by the SAR government); in the first nine months of 2026, 116 listings raised over HK$388 billion, more than doubling year on year and already exceeding the whole of last year (HKEX and SAR government data).

The indices moved the same way. The IMD World Competitiveness Yearbook 2026 ranks Hong Kong second in the world, behind only Singapore, its highest placing since 2019 and rising for a third straight year; the 40th Global Financial Centres Index keeps Hong Kong third worldwide, first in Asia-Pacific and first in fintech; the QS World University Rankings 2027 place five Hong Kong universities in the global top hundred, HKU at 11 and first in Asia, CUHK at 18, HKUST at 33, PolyU at 50, CityU at 52. The talent admission schemes have drawn over 310,000 professionals to Hong Kong (as of October 2026), more than 110,000 of them under the Top Talent Pass Scheme. The Heritage Foundation's Index of Economic Freedom, which ranked Hong Kong first in the world for twenty-five consecutive years from 1995 to 2019, removed the city's separate entry after the NSL on the stated ground that it is "controlled from Beijing" — when an index expunges a city for a political judgment, it advertises its own political character.

A city with eight consecutive quarters of growth, IPOs back on top of the world in 2025, the world's second-best competitiveness and five universities in the top hundred is precisely the city certain politicians and organisations were so eager to declare "dead". Their "disappointment" with Hong Kong is not because Hong Kong's rule of law failed — it is because Hong Kong's development bankrupted their narrative. A bankrupt narrative needs fresh financing, and "martyred democracy fighter" is the cheapest story going. What they need, then, is timing rather than truth: they knew the sentence would come (the guilty-plea process was public, and the statutory tariff was readable by anyone), and they knew it would be available (because any country with national security laws would sentence the same conduct) — they simply wrote the sentencing date into the script and waited for it to arrive on cue.

The SAR government spokesperson's reply deserves quoting once: for external forces to demand the "unconditional release of Joshua Wong and the dropping of the charges" is itself a grave assault on the rule of law.

Procedurally, the case is closed: the facts were found in open court, the plea stands on the record, ten years sits inside the statutory bracket, and the right of appeal remains.

Inside the courtroom, the business is done; outside, the play runs on its own touring schedule. The ten foreign ministries, the US Secretary of State and the congressional committees, Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch — the same names that filed past the verdict within twenty-four hours — are still issuing statements to an appointment calendar, the standing line unchanged: release immediately and unconditionally. The case has closed its run; they will not take their bow.

The city will not join that show. Publishing next quarter's numbers on time is the whole of its reply.

Those numbers answer a second question along the way: since the NSL restored public order, the economy has grown eight quarters running, IPOs have reclaimed first place in the world, and five universities hold places in the global top hundred. Whether the space of "two systems" is wide enough is something the reports state better than the statements.

The sentence is in the courtroom, the numbers in the quarterly reports: both checkable, both built to last. The statements touch neither. The Desk, as always, will keep watching the one that is checkable.

On 24 November the Government announced that the Support Fund for Wang Fuk Court in Tai Po would roll out two new beginning support grants: HK$300,000 for each flat owner, paid in two instalments, HK$150,000 at the end of December and HK$150,000 at the end of June next year; and HK$200,000 for the family of each person who died, paid in a single instalment at the end of December. Neither grant requires an application; both reach residents through the Social Welfare Department's "one social worker per household" mechanism. The press release says this follows the Chief Executive's instruction, so that owners and the bereaved can meet the needs of a new stage and begin a new life. The arrangement is sound. The grants reach residents directly, without application; the ceremonies are left to community bodies to hold. For residents setting out on a new life, this is the most practical of starting points. Two days remain before the first anniversary of the fire, at a moment when the whole city is watching how the aftermath is handled. At such a moment, the same deed, already done, wins wider acceptance the more exactly its details are told, and breeds more speculation the more vaguely it is told. At this stage of the aftermath the money has been paid; what remains is to tell what has been done exactly, and to settle what has not yet been decided. What follows are suggestions on two matters: the accounts of the support fund, and the use of the site.

The fund's total is about HK$4.8 billion, of which the Government put in only HK$300 million as seed money and the remaining HK$4.5 billion came from public donations, nearly 300,000 of them. Donations of HK$50,000 or less number more than 296,000, over nine in ten of all gifts, but amount to only about HK$430 million, or 9.5 per cent of the total; donations of HK$1 million or more are under 1 per cent of the gifts yet come close to HK$4 billion, 84 per cent, mainly from businesses in Hong Kong and on the mainland and from overseas Chinese associations. In other words, the money is at once the small change of several hundred thousand ordinary people and the largesse of a few big companies. The Government explained this structure to the media on 23 November, and explained it clearly; but media reports last a day, and most citizens never heard it. What citizens do not hear, they guess: some assume the grants are all taxpayers' money, others assume that big donors can dictate arrangements. Neither guess matches the facts, and there is only one way to dispel them, which is to tell the structure again, in full. It is suggested that, when the fund's report goes to the Legislative Council, the Government also publish a plain summary of income and expenditure, so that a citizen who gave HK$500 can see which measure that gift went to.

Close to three-fifths of the fund, about HK$2.8 billion, is reserved to support the Government's acquisition of every flat in Wang Fuk Court; the remaining HK$2 billion goes to residents' living needs, covering the thirteen emergency and medium-term measures introduced over the past year, such as a one-off grant of HK$100,000, a rental grant of HK$300,000 over two years, a relocation grant of HK$50,000, and special grants to the bereaved, the injured, students and domestic helpers; then the two new grants, about HK$630 million, and the extension of the rental grant. On 23 November the Government told the media that, after counting committed spending and a small buffer, the fund is "seven or eight parts used" (back-translated), and that any further large need might require fresh public money. "Seven or eight parts" is a candid phrase, but it is also a vague number, and vague numbers invite guessing. In fact the rental grant alone, at HK$300,000 for each of the 1,984 flats in the eight blocks, commits nearly HK$6 billion, far beyond the HK$2 billion the fund can spend on support; in April the Government opened five advance accounts to cover last year's relief measures, with a ceiling of HK$1.16 billion. That is to say, a substantial share of the money disbursed in the fund's name has in practice been advanced from public funds. If this layer is left unexplained, people outside will each draw their own conclusions, and the theories will multiply: that donations were diverted to the acquisition, that the Government is using donations to patch its own budget. It is suggested that the report set this out plainly: which measures are paid from donations, which from public funds, how much has been advanced, and whether it will be returned to the fund. Replace "seven or eight parts" with an exact percentage and an exact sum, and speculation has nothing to feed on; told clearly, the numbers can only add to public trust in the Government, not subtract from it.

What the Government has spent on Wang Fuk Court over the year goes far beyond the support fund. The acquisition of titles is the largest item: HK$2.8 billion reserved by the fund, HK$4 billion set aside in the 2026-27 Budget, and a further HK$1 billion approved by the Finance Committee in July to bring Wang Chi House into the scheme, HK$7.8 billion in all, with owners who have received payment averaging more than HK$3 million each; there are also tax waivers for two years of assessment and free Chinese and Western medical consultations extended to the end of April. Counting public money already approved or advanced, the figure is about HK$6.5 billion, around 1 per cent of the HK$648.4 billion in operating expenditure budgeted for 2026-27; counting donations and all commitments, the ceiling is about HK$17.3 billion, or 2.7 per cent. For a fire that took 168 lives, this spending is right, and the public by and large accepts it. Yet until now these figures have been scattered across a dozen releases; even reporters who follow the story must piece them together to see the whole, and the versions they piece together naturally differ. This is precisely the case of the vaguer the telling, the more the guessing. It is suggested that the Government publish a single year-end summary, listing acquisition, grants, waivers and advances together, each item with its source and date. This is no extra burden; it is telling clearly what has already been done. One exact list is more persuasive than ten scattered releases, and it holds up better under outside scrutiny.

One sentence in the press release deserves credit. In explaining why the families of the dead receive a further HK$200,000, the Government states plainly that some of the deceased were not flat owners. The sentence shows a careful eye for the bereaved, and it shows that the Government itself understands the value of giving reasons. Following the same line of thought, one can think one step further. Owners now have the HK$300,000 new grant, HK$300,000 in rental grants, HK$50,000 for relocation, HK$100,000 as a one-off grant, and more than HK$3 million from the acquisition. Tenants who lived in Wang Fuk Court that night received HK$50,000 for relocation in December last year; the two new grants are addressed to owners and to the families of the dead. Tenants lost their homes just the same, and lived through that night just the same. If the scope of the grants is left unexplained, this is the point at which the guess "owners only" most easily takes hold, and once a guess has formed, explaining it away costs twice the effort. The Government might consider one of two courses: either add a new-life grant for tenants, at an amount need not match the owners'; or state the reason tenants are outside this round, and the existing channels through which they can still be supported. Whichever course is taken, a specific account will stop the guessing.

The use of the site is the matter in the aftermath least worth leaving to drift. On the morning of 24 November the Chief Secretary said on radio that the Government would not organise a memorial event, as many events, including those of religious bodies, were already being held by the community; the Government would not obstruct lawful mourning and would, in some cases, help with transport. This choice follows the same reasoning as the support fund: the community holds the ceremony, the Government supplies money, effort and order. Grief is kept within bounds and life goes on; this is a practical approach. Yet once the ceremony is handed to the community, what remains on the site becomes a matter that must be planned early. The authorities judge it highly likely that the seven blocks will be demolished in the long run, the remaining titles are to be resumed by legislation next year, and what is built there is a decision the Government must make; last December the Hong Kong Institute of Surveyors proposed a memorial park, in February lawmakers proposed a memorial park, a temple and a museum, and the Deputy Financial Secretary said at the time, "we are willing to consider different possibilities" (back-translated). Nine months on, the site's future is still those three words, "willing to consider"; and a plot left empty and unexplained most readily invites guesses, that the Government wishes to handle it quietly, or intends to let it be forgotten. Such guesses do no good to the bereaved, to the neighbourhood, or to the Government. It is suggested that the Government use the time before demolition to fix a timetable for the site's planning, and give priority in that plan to a fire-safety education hall: where students come for lessons, where owners and owners' corporations learn the rules of building maintenance, where the 168 names have a dignified place, and which the bereaved help to design. One of the bereaved has said that "every Hongkonger must remember" this fire (back-translated); if the site can teach people to prevent fire and to escape it, then what is remembered is the lesson, and what is carried away is the ability to go forward. After the Wenchuan earthquake, 12 May became Disaster Prevention and Reduction Day; what 26 November becomes is likewise worth the Government's consideration, and worth stating early.

Until now, most of the fund's figures have been spoken by officials meeting the media; spoken words last a day in the reports, and numbers warp as they are retold. A steadier course is to put these figures into formal published documents: beyond the press release, a dedicated web page setting out the fund's income and expenditure summary, the year-end totals, and the application arrangements and payment dates for each grant, in tables and charts, with a set of frequently asked questions answering the things citizens ask most, where the donations came from, where the money went, whether tenants are covered, and when the site will be decided. Documents can be checked and accumulated; whenever a citizen opens them, the same set of numbers is there. At the same time, it is suggested that the Government actively supply this complete material to the media and hold briefings, so that newspapers, radio, television and online platforms of different outlooks all receive the same accurate file. The fuller the material the media receive, the more their reporting shares a common factual basis; the more fragmentary it is, the more each outlet must guess for itself. This is not an invitation to the media to say kind things; it is to let all reporting start from the same set of facts.

To draw these threads together, four suggestions follow, and all four come down to one idea: turn the vague into the exact. First, when the fund's report goes to the Legislative Council, attach the figures added since the cut-off date, replace "seven or eight parts" with an exact percentage, and set out what each part of the HK$4.5 billion in donations paid for and how public money and donations are actually shared. Second, publish a year-end summary listing acquisition, grants, waivers and advances together, and explain the arrangements for tenants. Third, publish a timetable for the site, replacing "willing to consider" with an exact date, and state whether a fire-safety education facility is included, whether the names of the 168 dead will have a dignified place, and whether the bereaved will help with the design. Fourth, publish all of the above in formal documents and on a dedicated web page with a set of frequently asked questions, and actively supply complete material to the media. None of the four needs extra resources; each needs only that what has already been be told systematically and with numbers.

The finer the detail, the wider the acceptance; the vaguer the telling, the more the guessing.
